L01|NARRATOR|So who is he? Not a virus. Not a bacterium. Malaria is a parasite: a tiny single-celled creature called Plasmodium. And he can't travel on his own.
L02|NARRATOR|He needs a ride: a mosquito called Anopheles. And only the females bite. They need blood to make their eggs.
L03|NARRATOR|When she bites, he slips into your blood and goes straight to the liver. There he hides and multiplies, quietly, for a week or two.

L08|NARRATOR|But where did he come from? Malaria parasites are ancient. They infect birds, lizards, monkeys and apes. The deadliest kind in humans, Plasmodium falciparum, most likely jumped to us from gorillas in Africa.
L09|NARRATOR|When people began to farm, clearing forests and digging ponds and ditches, the mosquitoes followed. He spread across Africa, then into Asia and Europe.

S01|NARRATOR|So what does he do to you? It begins with the cold. You shiver so hard your teeth chatter, under blankets, in the heat.

S03|NARRATOR|Until it starts again. Every second or third day, as a new wave of blood cells bursts. The Romans had names for these fevers: tertian and quartan.
S04|NARRATOR|He destroys so many red blood cells that you turn pale and weak. Your spleen swells. And the deadliest kind can clog the tiny blood vessels of the brain. Coma. Death.

L13|NARRATOR|People have described his fevers for thousands of years. Ancient Chinese and Indian texts, and the Greek doctor Hippocrates, wrote about regular fevers and swollen spleens. And they all noticed something: it was worse near marshes.
