C03|NARRATOR|Everyone blames the Russian winter. But in 2001, builders in Vilnius dug up a mass grave of Napoleon's soldiers. Inside their teeth, scientists found the real killer.
C04|TYPHUS|The winter gets all the credit. Typical. I did all the work.
L01|NARRATOR|So who is he? A bacterium, Rickettsia prowazekii. Tiny, and so helpless that it can only live inside other cells. And it needs a partner: the body louse.
L02|NARRATOR|The louse drinks the blood of an infected person and gets infected itself. Then it moves to a new person, and leaves its droppings on the skin. The person scratches the itch, and rubs the bacteria right into the bite.
L03|NARRATOR|Lice love crowds, cold and dirt: people who can't wash or change their clothes. Soldiers. Prisoners. Refugees. Sailors. The starving.
L04|TYPHUS|Give me a war, a famine, a crowded jail. I'll do the rest.
L05|NARRATOR|Where did he come from? Rickettsia bacteria are ancient. They have lived inside insects and ticks for millions of years. But his partner, the body louse, is younger. It only appeared when humans started wearing clothes, perhaps a hundred thousand years ago.
L06|NARRATOR|His name comes from the Greek word typhos: smoke, or haze. Because his victims fall into a foggy daze. And no, he's not typhoid. Different germ, different disease. Same confusing name.
L07|TYPHUS|Typhus. Typhoid. People mix us up all the time. Honestly, it's insulting.
S01|NARRATOR|So what does he do to you? About two weeks after the bite: a sudden high fever, a crushing headache, and pain in every muscle.
S02|NARRATOR|A few days later, a rash appears on the chest and spreads outward, often everywhere except the face, palms and soles.
S03|NARRATOR|Then comes the haze. Patients become confused, delirious, staring into nothing. In the old days, up to four in ten died, often from failing hearts or brains.
S04|NARRATOR|And even if you survive, he can hide in your body for years, and wake up again when you're old. Doctors call it Brill-Zinsser disease.
S05|TYPHUS|Fever, rash, a lovely fog in the head. And then, when you least expect it, I come back.
L08|NARRATOR|His first clear appearance in history: Spain, 1489. During the siege of Granada, the Spanish army lost about three thousand men in battle, and around seventeen thousand to a strange new fever.
L09|NARRATOR|From then on he followed every army. He killed more soldiers than bullets did in war after war. They called him camp fever, war fever, ship fever.
L10|NARRATOR|And jail fever. In 1577, at a trial in Oxford, sick prisoners were brought into a packed courtroom. Within weeks, the judge, the sheriff and hundreds of others were dead. It became known as the Black Assize.
L11|TYPHUS|One trial, and I sentenced the judge. Now that's justice.
L12|NARRATOR|Then 1812. Napoleon's soldiers marched in summer heat, packed together, unwashed, wearing the same clothes for weeks. Typhus spread through the ranks before they even reached Moscow.
L13|NARRATOR|On the retreat, it got worse. Soldiers crowded into any shelter, stripped the coats from the dead, and the lice moved with the coats. In Vilnius, thousands died in a few weeks.
L14|TYPHUS|Napoleon conquered half of Europe. I conquered Napoleon.
L15|NARRATOR|In 1847, during Ireland's Great Famine, starving families crowded into workhouses and onto ships to America. So many died on the way that people called them coffin ships.
L16|NARRATOR|On Grosse Île, a quarantine island in Canada, more than five thousand of them are buried. Many of the doctors and nurses who cared for them died too.
L17|NARRATOR|Then a French doctor in Tunisia, Charles Nicolle, noticed something odd. Typhus patients infected people outside the hospital. But once they had been bathed and given clean clothes at the door, they infected no one.
L18|NARRATOR|So it wasn't the patient. It was something on the patient. In 1909 he proved it: the louse. He won the Nobel Prize in 1928.
L19|NARRATOR|Two scientists who studied him, Howard Ricketts and Stanislaus von Prowazek, both died of typhus. The bacterium was named after them.
