L01|NARRATOR|So who is he? A bacterium. Mycobacterium leprae, a close cousin of tuberculosis. In 1873, a Norwegian doctor, Gerhard Armauer Hansen, spotted him under the microscope. He was one of the very first germs ever linked to a human disease.
L02|NARRATOR|And he is slow. Incredibly slow. He divides only about once every two weeks. And after a hundred and fifty years, scientists still can't grow him in a lab dish.
L03|NARRATOR|He loves the cold. He settles in the coolest parts of the body: the skin, the nose, the ears, the hands and feet. And above all, the nerves.
L04|LEPROSY|Warm hearts don't interest me. I prefer cold hands.
L05|NARRATOR|Strangely, he is one of the hardest diseases to catch. He spreads through droplets from the nose and mouth, usually after months of close contact. And about ninety-five percent of people are naturally immune.
L06|NARRATOR|But he is patient. After infection, he can wait five years, sometimes twenty, before the first sign appears.
L07|LEPROSY|Twenty years? That's nothing. I have all the time in the world.
L08|NARRATOR|Where did he come from? His family is ancient. He may have started in East Africa or the Near East, and travelled with humans for tens of thousands of years. The oldest known case is a skeleton from India, about four thousand years old.
L09|NARRATOR|Along the way, he threw away almost half of his own genes. He can no longer survive on his own. He needs us.
L10|NARRATOR|And he has a few other friends. In the southern United States, nine-banded armadillos carry him, and can pass him to people. In Britain, even some red squirrels have him.
L11|LEPROSY|Armadillos. Squirrels. I'm not fussy about my company.
S01|NARRATOR|So what does he do to you? It starts quietly. A pale or reddish patch on the skin. It doesn't itch. It doesn't hurt. And if you touch it, you can barely feel it.
S02|NARRATOR|Because he attacks the nerves. Slowly, the hands and feet go numb. You can grab a hot pot and not notice. You can walk all day with a stone in your shoe.
S03|NARRATOR|Those unnoticed injuries get infected, again and again. Over many years, fingers and toes can become shortened. They don't simply fall off. That's a myth. The damage comes from wounds that no one could feel.
S04|NARRATOR|The nerves of the face can fail too. The eyes can't blink properly and slowly go blind. In the worst form, the skin of the face thickens into lumps.
S05|NARRATOR|He almost never kills you directly. What he takes is feeling. And then, very often, your place among other people.
S06|LEPROSY|I rarely kill anyone. I simply make sure nobody wants to touch you.
L12|NARRATOR|People have known him for thousands of years. In ancient India, the surgeon Sushruta described the disease and treated it with oil pressed from the seeds of the chaulmoogra tree.
L13|NARRATOR|In the Bible, the Hebrew word 'tzaraat' was translated as leprosy. It probably covered many different skin conditions. But the name stuck, and so did the fear.
L14|LEPROSY|They gave my name to every rash in the world. I took it as a compliment.
L15|NARRATOR|In medieval Europe, he was everywhere. Thousands of leper houses, named lazar houses after Saint Lazarus, were built just outside the towns.
L16|NARRATOR|In some places, a person diagnosed was led into church for a ceremony like a funeral. Afterwards, they had to wear special clothes and gloves, and carry a bell or a wooden clapper to warn people they were coming.
L17|NARRATOR|They couldn't enter the market, touch the food, or drink from a shared well. They were still alive, but treated as if they had already died.
L18|LEPROSY|Ring the bell, everybody moves away. That bell was my favourite invention.
L19|NARRATOR|He even reached a throne. Baldwin the Fourth of Jerusalem was a boy when his tutor noticed he felt no pain when the other boys pinched his arm. He became king at thirteen. At sixteen, he defeated Saladin at the Battle of Montgisard. He died at twenty-three.

L29|NARRATOR|For centuries, the best treatment was still chaulmoogra oil. Swallowed, it made people sick. Injected, it formed painful lumps. Then, in 1915, a twenty-three-year-old chemist in Hawaii, Alice Ball, found a way to make it injectable.
L30|NARRATOR|She died the next year, at just twenty-four. A university president published her work under his own name. Her credit was only restored decades later.
