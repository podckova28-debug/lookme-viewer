# The ENTIRE History of Mead — script v2 (retention rewrite)

Format: animated history explainer, ~8 min, one narrator, no villain. 9 parts (p0…p8).
Visual style: white background, detailed ink-and-watercolour characters (like the current disease thumbnails),
plus a simple white round-headed stick figure = "you". Handwritten marker labels and arrows for jokes and facts.
Tone: warm, dry humour, second person in the story scenes.

Retention structure:
- Cold open: a scene + the central mystery ("the oldest drink on Earth… so why has almost nobody tasted it?").
- That question is the spine; the answer is promised, teased mid-video, and paid off in p7.
- Every part = a "you" scene or a surprising fact, with a small hook into the next part.
- A running gag (the bee) and two callbacks (the goat, the hollow tree) reward people who keep watching.

=== p0 ===
You are a Viking. It's the year nine hundred. Your king has just handed you a horn of golden mead in front of the whole hall. Everyone is watching. You drink it. Delicious. Congratulations. You now owe him your life. Literally. Because for thousands of years, this was not just a drink. It was a contract, a gift from the gods, and maybe the very first alcohol humans ever made. And yet, today, most people have never tasted it. So what happened? How does the oldest drink on Earth end up as something you only see in video games? To find out, we need to go back. Way back. Before the Vikings. Before beer. Possibly before farming. To a tree.

=== p1 ===
Somewhere in a forest, thousands of years ago, there's a hollow tree. Inside it, a wild beehive full of honey. It rains. Water drips into the hive. Now, honey on its own never goes bad. It's too thick and too sweet for anything to grow in it. But add water, and wild yeast moves in. The yeast eats the sugar and turns it into alcohol. A few warm days later, that tree smells… suspicious. And sooner or later, a hungry human climbs up, sticks a hand in, takes a taste… and has a very interesting afternoon. Many historians think this is how our ancestors met alcohol. They didn't invent it. They found it. In a tree. Which, honestly, is the best possible way to discover anything.

=== p2 ===
And our ancestors were obsessed with honey. On a cliff in eastern Spain, in the Cave of the Spider, there's a painting thousands of years old. A tiny figure hangs from ropes, reaching into a bees' nest while the bees swarm around. That's the risk people took for something sweet. Now jump to China, around seven thousand BC. In a village called Jiahu, archaeologists dug up clay jars. Chemists tested what was stuck inside. Rice. Fruit. And honey. Fermented. It's one of the oldest alcoholic drinks ever found. Nine thousand years old. Older than the pyramids by about four thousand years. And honey was in the recipe from day one.

=== p3 ===
Here's a weird clue to how old this drink is. Look at the word. In old English: meodu. In Sanskrit, the ancient language of India: madhu, which means honey. In ancient Greek: methy, which means wine, or anything that gets you drunk. Three languages, thousands of kilometres apart, and the same word. Linguists think it comes from one single ancestor language, spoken more than five thousand years ago, before it split into Greek, Indian, Germanic and dozens more. Which means people were saying something like "medhu" before anyone had invented writing. The word for this drink is older than the alphabet.

=== p4 ===
But nobody took mead as seriously as the Vikings. Up north, grapes don't grow. But bees? Plenty. So mead became the drink of gods. In Norse myth, the gods brewed a mead from the blood of the wisest being who ever lived. One sip, and you became a poet. A giant stole it. So Odin, king of the gods, snuck in, drank all of it, turned into an eagle and flew home with the mead in his mouth. The giant chased him as an eagle too. And in the panic, a few drops fell. The gods said those drops became bad poetry. And in Valhalla, the hall of dead heroes, a goat stands on the roof, and mead flows from her udder. Forever. Remember the goat. Hold that thought.

=== p5 ===
Back to your horn of mead. In the Viking world, and in Anglo-Saxon England, the mead hall was everything. In the poem Beowulf, the king's hall is where warriors eat, sleep, get their gold, and where a monster called Grendel comes to eat them. Accepting the king's mead meant one thing: when the fighting starts, you're first in line. An old Welsh poem tells of three hundred warriors who feasted on mead for a whole year, then marched into a hopeless battle. Almost none came back. The poem says they paid for their mead. So yes. Your free drink. Very expensive. And that goat in Valhalla? That was the dream. Endless free mead, and nobody ever sends you the bill.

=== p6 ===
Now, you've probably heard that newlyweds used to drink mead for a whole month to bring luck, and that's where the word honeymoon comes from. Great story. Almost certainly not true. "Honeymoon" originally meant that marriage is sweetest at first and then fades, like the moon. Ouch. But here's what is true. In the Middle Ages, the biggest beekeepers in Europe were monks. Not mainly for mead. For candles. Churches burned beeswax by the ton, and the monks made mead from what was left over. Which brings us back to our mystery. Because in the fifteen thirties, something happened that would hit mead hard.

=== p7 ===
England's King Henry the Eighth broke with the Pope and shut down the monasteries. All of them. In just a few years. And with the monks went a lot of the beekeeping. Then came sugar. Cheap cane sugar from the colonies, grown and harvested by enslaved people, poured into Europe and replaced honey in kitchens. Beer was cheaper to brew. Wine was easier to ship and sell. And mead, which needs a lot of bees, a lot of work and a lot of patience, slowly lost. Not in one battle. In a thousand small ones. The drink of gods and kings became something your great-grandmother made in a jar in the cellar. So that's the answer. Mead didn't die. It got priced out.

=== p8 ===
But it never fully disappeared. In Ethiopia, people still drink tej, a golden honey wine served at weddings in a round glass flask. In Poland, family recipes survived, and the European Union now protects the names of traditional Polish meads. And lately, mead is back. Hundreds of small meaderies have opened in the last twenty years. Partly because Vikings are cool again. Movies, shows and video games put a horn of mead in everyone's hand. So the next time you see one, remember: that drink is older than the Vikings, older than writing, and maybe older than every other drink on Earth. It started in a hollow tree. Would you try it? Tell me in the comments. And next time: the drink sailors were paid in, and pirates fought over. Rum.

## Notes and sources to verify before recording
- Jiahu, Henan, China: fermented beverage of rice, honey and fruit, c. 7000 BC — McGovern et al., PNAS 2004. Script says "one of the oldest", not "the oldest": earlier beer-like residues (Raqefet Cave, c. 11000 BC) are claimed but debated. Thumbnail must not say "older than beer" as fact.
- Pyramids: Great Pyramid c. 2560 BC → Jiahu is ~4,500 years older ("about four thousand" is safe).
- Cuevas de la Araña (Bicorp, Valencia): honey-gathering painting, usually c. 8,000 years old (uncertain) — script: "thousands of years old".
- PIE *médʰu → OE meodu, Skt madhu, Gk methy; PIE c. 4500–2500 BC → "more than five thousand years ago" is within range; writing (Sumer) c. 3200 BC — "older than the alphabet" is safe (alphabet c. 1800 BC).
- Norse Mead of Poetry: Kvasir's blood, Suttungr, Odin as eagle, spilled drops = "the poetaster's share" — Snorri, Skáldskaparmál. Heiðrún the goat — Grímnismál / Gylfaginning.
- Beowulf: Heorot, Grendel.
- Y Gododdin: ~300 warriors, a year of mead, battle of Catraeth, "paid for their mead".
- Honeymoon: attested 1540s; sense = love sweetest at first, waning like the moon. Mead story = folk etymology.
- Dissolution of the Monasteries: 1536–1541, Henry VIII after the break with Rome.
- Sugar from slave plantations replacing honey in Europe — 17th–18th c.
- Tej (Ethiopia), berele flask. Polish meads: EU TSG 2008. US/EU meadery growth: "hundreds", no exact number.
