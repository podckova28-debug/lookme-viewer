# The ENTIRE History of Mead — script v1

Format: animated history explainer, ~9 min, one narrator, no villain. 9 parts (p0…p8), same as the other long-form channels.
Tone: warm, curious, a little dry humour. Facts marked [src] in the notes at the bottom.

=== p0 ===
Nine thousand years ago, in a village in what is now China, someone mixed rice, fruit and honey in a clay jar, and left it alone for a while. When they came back, the jar was bubbling. And the drink inside made everyone feel very, very good. That might be the oldest alcoholic drink we have ever found. Long before whisky. Long before wine became a business. Before almost everything. It was made with honey. Today, most people have never tasted it. They know it from Viking movies and video games. But for thousands of years, this drink was poured for gods, kings and heroes. This is the entire history of mead.

=== p1 ===
First, what is it? Mead is honey, water and yeast. That's it. Mix honey with water, let wild yeast find it, and the yeast eats the sugar and turns it into alcohol. Honey on its own doesn't spoil. It's too thick and too sweet for anything to grow in it. But add water, and it wakes up. Which means mead may be one of the easiest drinks in the world to make by accident. A beehive in a hollow tree fills with rainwater. A few warm days later, the inside of that tree smells suspiciously like a party. Some historians think our ancestors discovered alcohol exactly like that. Not by inventing it. By finding it.

=== p2 ===
And they were already chasing honey. On a cliff in eastern Spain, in the Cave of the Spider, there's a painting thousands of years old. A tiny human figure hangs from ropes, reaching into a wild bees' nest, while the bees swarm around them. Honey was the sweetest thing our ancestors could find. Getting it meant climbing cliffs and getting stung. So you can understand why nobody wanted to waste a single drop. Then, at a Neolithic village called Jiahu, in China, archaeologists found pottery jars around nine thousand years old. Chemists scraped the residue from the inside. They found traces of rice, honey and fruit. A fermented drink. Not pure mead, but honey was right there in the recipe, at the very beginning of the story of alcohol.

=== p3 ===
Here's a clue to how old mead really is. Look at the word. In old English it was "meodu". In Sanskrit, the ancient language of India, "madhu" means honey. In ancient Greek, "methy" meant wine, the thing that makes you drunk. Linguists think all of these come from one very old word, spoken thousands of years ago by people whose languages later spread from India to Ireland. So before there was a Greek, a Viking or an Indian, there may have been a word for this drink. In ancient India, sacred texts praise the honey-sweet drink of the gods. In ancient Greece, honey and wine were mixed together in religious rituals. And the Roman writer Pliny described a honey drink made with rainwater, aged for years until it tasted like old wine.

=== p4 ===
But nobody loved mead like the people of the north. In Northern Europe, grapes barely grow. Barley was for bread. But honey? There was honey. So mead became the drink of chieftains and warriors. In the old Norse myths, mead was literally magic. The gods made a mead from the blood of the wisest being in the world. Whoever drank it became a poet. The god Odin stole it from a giant, turned into an eagle, and flew back to the gods with the mead in his mouth. That's why, for the Vikings, poetry itself was called "the mead of the poets". And in Valhalla, the hall of fallen heroes, a magical goat stood on the roof, eating leaves from a tree. From her udder flowed mead. Enough for every warrior, forever.

=== p5 ===
And the mead hall was the centre of the world. In the old English poem Beowulf, the king builds a great mead hall called Heorot. That's where the warriors feast, where the king hands out gold, and where the monster Grendel attacks. Sharing the king's mead wasn't just a party. It was a contract. You drank his mead, and in return you fought for him. Literally. There's a famous old Welsh poem about a band of warriors who feasted on mead for a whole year before marching into a hopeless battle. Almost all of them died. The poem says they paid for their mead. Today, people still say a tired joke: that newlyweds once drank mead for a month to bring luck and children, and that's why we call it a honeymoon. It's a nice story. Historians think it's probably made up.

=== p6 ===
So what happened? Why isn't mead everywhere today? First, honey was expensive. To make a lot of mead, you need a lot of bees, and a lot of work. In medieval Europe, monasteries kept huge numbers of bees. Not mainly for mead. For candles. Churches burned beeswax, and monks made mead from what was left. When England shut down its monasteries in the fifteen thirties, a lot of that beekeeping disappeared with them. Then came sugar. Cheap cane sugar from the colonies replaced honey in kitchens. Beer was cheaper to brew. Wine could travel. Mead slowly turned from the drink of kings into something your grandmother made in the countryside.

=== p7 ===
But it never fully disappeared. In Ethiopia, people still drink tej, a golden honey wine flavoured with the leaves of a local shrub. It's served at weddings and celebrations, in a round glass flask with a long neck, and it's been part of life there for centuries. In Poland, mead survived in old family recipes. Polish meads are even sorted by how much honey is in them, from light to extremely strong and sweet, and the European Union protects their traditional names. In Lithuania and Russia, there are honey drinks too, some barely alcoholic, some very much not. Each of them is a little piece of that very old story.

=== p8 ===
And now, mead is coming back. In the last twenty years, hundreds of small meaderies have opened across the United States and Europe. Part of the reason? Vikings are cool again. TV shows, films and video games put a horn of mead into everyone's imagination. People tried it, and found out it's not just one drink. It can be dry or sweet, still or sparkling, with fruit, spices or hops. So, the next time you see a Viking raise a horn in a movie, remember: that drink is older than the Vikings, older than the gods they prayed to, and maybe older than almost every other drink on Earth. Would you try it? Tell me in the comments. And next time: the drink that sailors were paid in, and pirates fought over. Rum.

## Notes and sources to verify before recording
- Jiahu, Henan, China: fermented beverage of rice, honey and fruit (hawthorn/grape), c. 7000 BC — McGovern et al., PNAS 2004. "Might be the oldest alcoholic drink we have found" is hedged on purpose: older beer-like residues (Raqefet Cave, Israel, c. 11000 BC) are claimed but debated. **So the thumbnail line "older than beer" is NOT safe** — suggest "the first drink?" or "9,000 years old" instead.
- Cuevas de la Araña (Bicorp, Valencia): honey-gathering rock painting, usually dated c. 8,000 years ago (dating uncertain) — script says "thousands of years old".
- PIE *médʰu → OE meodu, Skt madhu (honey), Gk methy (wine) — standard etymology.
- Pliny the Elder, Natural History, book 14: hydromel/aged honey drink with rainwater.
- Norse Mead of Poetry (Kvasir's blood, Suttungr, Odin as eagle) — Snorri's Prose Edda. Heiðrún the goat on Valhalla's roof, mead from her udder — Grímnismál / Gylfaginning.
- Beowulf: Heorot, the mead hall.
- Welsh poem: Y Gododdin — warriors feast on mead for a year before the battle of Catraeth; "paid for their mead".
- Honeymoon mead story: popular myth; "honeymoon" attested in English from the 1540s, original sense = sweet first month that wanes.
- Dissolution of the Monasteries in England: 1536–1541.
- Tej (Ethiopia): honey wine flavoured with gesho leaves, served in a berele flask.
- Polish meads (półtorak, dwójniak, trójniak, czwórniak) registered as EU Traditional Speciality Guaranteed in 2008.
- US mead revival: hundreds of meaderies (American Mead Makers Association figures) — keep "hundreds", no exact number.
