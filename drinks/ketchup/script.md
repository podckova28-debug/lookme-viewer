# The Entire History of KETCHUP — script v1 (mead formula)

Format: like mead, not absinthe. Dry humour in every part, second-person "you" scenes, cartoon gag frames,
fast cuts (new picture every 2–3 s), animated clips on key frames. One sentence per line (line index = shot cue).
Running gag: "Where are the tomatoes?" — a stick figure waits with a tomato (p0, p1, p2, payoff p3).
Callbacks: the fish sauce (p1 → p8), the pills (p4 → p8). Ending teases the hamburger (next food episode).
Numbers spelled out for TTS. Target ~6–7 min, 9 parts.

=== p0 ===
You are a sailor on a British ship.
It's the year sixteen ninety, and you've just landed in Southeast Asia.
A merchant hands you a little jar of brown, salty, slightly terrifying sauce.
You smell it.
It smells like fish.
Because it is fish.
Fermented fish.
And he tells you what it's called.
Kay-chap.
Congratulations.
You have just met ketchup.
No tomatoes.
No sugar.
No bottle.
Today, the world eats hundreds of millions of bottles of the red stuff every year.
So how did a fish sauce from Asia turn into the thing on your fries?
And where, exactly, are the tomatoes?
Spoiler.
They're late.
Very late.

=== p1 ===
The story starts on the coast of southern China and Southeast Asia.
Fishermen packed small fish and salt into big jars, and left them in the sun.
For months.
What came out was a dark, salty liquid, full of flavour, that made plain rice taste amazing.
In one Chinese dialect, the sauce was called something like kay-chap.
British and Dutch traders loved it.
It didn't go bad on long voyages.
It made boring ship food taste like something.
So they took it home.
Where they immediately discovered a problem.
Nobody in England knew how to make it.

=== p2 ===
So English cooks did what English cooks do.
They improvised.
A cookbook from seventeen twenty seven tells you to make ketchup from anchovies, shallots, vinegar and white wine.
Other recipes used oysters.
Or walnuts.
Or mushrooms.
Mushroom ketchup became a big hit, a thin black sauce poured over meat and fish.
For about a hundred years, ketchup meant almost any salty, spicy sauce in a bottle.
Walnut ketchup.
Lemon ketchup.
Even beer ketchup.
And the tomatoes?
Still not here.
Mostly because a lot of people in Britain thought tomatoes might kill them.

=== p3 ===
Tomatoes come from the Americas.
When they arrived in Europe, plenty of people were suspicious.
They belong to the nightshade family, cousins of some genuinely poisonous plants.
Some people called them love apples, others called them poison apples.
A popular story says rich Europeans got sick after eating tomatoes off pewter plates, because the acid pulled lead out of the metal.
That's probably more legend than history.
But the reputation stuck.
Then, in eighteen twelve, in Philadelphia, a scientist named James Mease wrote down a recipe for ketchup made from love apples.
Tomatoes.
Finally.
The tomato has entered the chat.
And within twenty years, Americans would decide it could cure almost anything.

=== p4 ===
In the eighteen thirties, a doctor named John Cook Bennett announced that tomatoes could treat indigestion, diarrhoea, and even liver problems.
America went tomato crazy.
Businessmen started selling tomato pills.
Dr Miles' Compound Extract of Tomato.
A cure for everything, in a little box.
There was just one tiny issue.
When people tested the pills, a lot of them barely contained any tomato at all.
Mostly, they were laxatives.
So yes, the pills did do something.
Just not what it said on the box.
By the eighteen fifties, the tomato pill craze had collapsed.
But the tomato had become famous.
And ketchup was about to get a lot worse, before it got better.

=== p5 ===
By the late eighteen hundreds, factories were selling ketchup by the barrel.
And a lot of it was disgusting.
Some makers used the leftovers from tomato canning, the skins, the cores, the bits nobody wanted.
It went bad fast.
So they added preservatives, and dyes, to keep it red.
Some bottles had mould in them.
Some had things in them that were never meant to be food.
In Washington, a government chemist named Harvey Wiley got worried about all the chemicals in America's food.
So he set up an experiment.
He recruited young men to eat meals spiked with common preservatives, and watched what happened to them.
The newspapers called them the Poison Squad.
Yes, that was a real thing.
And ketchup was about to meet a man who loved it a little too much.

=== p6 ===
His name was Henry John Heinz.
He started out selling horseradish in clear glass jars, so customers could see there was nothing hidden in it.
In eighteen seventy six, his company started selling tomato ketchup.
And Heinz made a bet.
No dodgy preservatives.
Instead, he used ripe tomatoes, and a lot more vinegar and sugar to keep it fresh.
That's why ketchup is sweet and sour today.
He sold it in a clear glass bottle, so you could see it was clean and red.
In nineteen oh six, America passed its first big food safety law.
And Heinz was ready.
He even had a famous slogan.
Fifty seven varieties.
Which was not actually the number of products they made.
They already made more than sixty.
Heinz just liked the number.

=== p7 ===
Then there's the bottle.
That glass bottle is famous for one thing.
Nothing comes out.
That's because ketchup is a strange kind of liquid.
When it's sitting still, it's thick, almost solid.
When you push it, it suddenly gets runny.
Scientists have a name for that, but you already know it as the shake, shake, shake, splat.
Heinz says the trick is to tap the bottle on the neck, right where the fifty seven is.
Then, in nineteen eighty one, the American government proposed letting schools count ketchup as a vegetable in school lunches.
People were furious.
The idea was dropped.
But for a moment, ketchup almost became a vegetable.

=== p8 ===
Today, ketchup is everywhere.
On fries in America, on rice in the Philippines, on pasta in places where Italians would rather not talk about it.
In the Philippines, there's even banana ketchup, invented during the Second World War, when tomatoes were scarce.
And remember the fish sauce from the very beginning?
In Indonesia, the word kecap still means a dark sauce, like soy sauce.
No tomatoes.
After four hundred years, part of the world is still waiting for them.
So ketchup started as fermented fish, became mushrooms, then medicine, then a scandal, and finally the most famous sauce on Earth.
Would you have tried the tomato pills?
Tell me in the comments.
And next time: the thing you put ketchup on.
The hamburger.

## Notes and sources to verify
- Etymology: Hokkien kê-tsiap / kôe-chiap (fish brine); Malay kicap/kecap. English "catchup" 1690 (B.E., A New Dictionary of the Canting Crew: "Catchup, a high East-India Sauce"); "ketchup" 1711.
- Dan Jurafsky, The Language of Food (2014), ch. "Ketchup, Cocktails and Pirates" — fish sauce origin, British traders.
- Eliza Smith, The Compleat Housewife (1727): "English Katchup" with anchovies, shallots, white wine vinegar, white wine.
- Mushroom/walnut/oyster ketchups 18th–19th c.; lemon ketchup and "beer ketchup" recipes exist in 18th–19th c. cookbooks (check exact source before naming).
- Tomato suspicion in Britain; pewter-lead story is widely repeated but weakly evidenced → told as "probably more legend than history".
- James Mease, 1812, Philadelphia — first known tomato ketchup recipe ("love apples").
- John Cook Bennett, 1834 claims; Archibald Miles, "Dr. Miles' Compound Extract of Tomato"; tomato pill craze 1830s–1840s collapsed; pills found to contain laxatives, little tomato (Andrew F. Smith, The Tomato in America, 1994; Pure Ketchup, 1996).
- Late 19th c. commercial ketchup from canning scraps, coal-tar dyes, benzoate preservatives.
- Harvey W. Wiley, USDA chief chemist, "hygienic table trials" from 1902, press name "Poison Squad"; Pure Food and Drug Act 1906.
- H. J. Heinz: horseradish in clear glass (1869); ketchup 1876; preservative-free recipe with more vinegar/sugar and ripe tomatoes, early 1900s; "57 Varieties" 1896 (already >60 products; he liked 5 and 7).
- Ketchup = shear-thinning (non-Newtonian) fluid; Heinz advises tapping the "57" on the neck.
- 1981 USDA school-lunch proposal (ketchup/relish as vegetable), withdrawn after public outcry.
- Banana ketchup, Philippines, Maria Orosa, WWII era.
- Indonesian kecap manis/asin = soy-based sauces.
- TTS: "Kay-chap" written phonetically; Mease → "Mees"; Orosa not named; "diarrhoea" — check pronunciation; avoid "often".
