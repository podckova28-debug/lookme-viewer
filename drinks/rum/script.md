# The ENTIRE History of Rum — script v1

Format: same as mead (white background, ink + watercolour, stick-figure "you", handwritten notes). One narrator, no villain.
Target: 8+ min (mid-roll ads). 9 parts (p0…p8).

Retention structure:
- Cold open: POV scene + the central mystery ("how does trash become the drink of navies, pirates and presidents?").
- Promise in p1 ("remember that, it's coming back"), paid off in p6 (triangle trade) and p8 (the answer).
- Running thread: the Navy ration — opens in p0, explained in p4, ends in p8 (Black Tot Day).
- Channel callback: yeast from the mead episode. Myth-busting beats: "Yo ho ho", "the real McCoy".
- Slavery is told plainly, no jokes in those lines.

=== p0 ===
You are a sailor in the British Navy. It's the year seventeen forty. It's noon, and a whistle blows. Every man on the ship lines up, and an officer pours out your daily ration of rum. Half a pint. Every single day. And nobody thinks this is strange. But a hundred years earlier, this drink was trash. Literally. It was made from the sticky black waste that sugar planters threw away. So how does trash become the drink of navies, pirates and presidents? How does it help start a revolution, fuel the slave trade, and end up in your glass with a tiny umbrella? To find out, we need to go to an island. And to a plant that changed the world. Sugar.

=== p1 ===
Sugar cane is a giant grass, first grown in New Guinea thousands of years ago. For most of history, sugar was a luxury. A spice. Almost a medicine. Kings ate it. Everyone else used honey. Then, in fourteen ninety-three, on his second voyage, Christopher Columbus brought sugar cane to the Caribbean. And it grew. Very, very well. Within a century, the islands were turning into one giant sugar farm. And here's the dark part, and we're not going to skip it. The work was brutal. Cutting cane in the heat. Boiling it day and night. So the planters filled the fields with enslaved Africans, forced across the ocean by the millions. Europe got its sugar. They paid for it. Remember that. It's coming back.

=== p2 ===
Now, to make sugar, you boil cane juice until crystals form. What's left is a thick, black, bitter syrup. Molasses. Tons and tons of it. And nobody wanted it. Some planters fed it to animals. Some just dumped it. Then, sometime in the sixteen hundreds, someone tried an old trick. Mix the molasses with water. Let wild yeast move in. Yes, yeast again. If you watched our mead video, you know it will eat anything sweet. Then heat it in a still, and catch the vapour. And out came… something. On Barbados, around sixteen fifty, a visitor described it as "a hot, hellish and terrible liquor." The locals called it kill-devil. Or rumbullion, which might be old English slang for a brawl. Either way, the name got shorter. Rum.

=== p3 ===
And early rum really was terrible. Harsh, cloudy, and sometimes made in copper pots that could poison you. But it had three huge advantages. It was cheap, because it was made from garbage. It was strong. And it did not spoil. On a long voyage, water turned green. Beer turned sour. Rum just sat there in its barrel, getting better. Which made it the perfect drink for people who spent their whole lives on ships. It was so useful, it even became money. In early Australia, coins were scarce, so people paid for work, land and favours in rum. In eighteen oh eight, the governor tried to crack down on the rum trade, and army officers arrested him. History calls it the Rum Rebellion. The governor's name? William Bligh. The same captain whose crew had already mutinied against him on the Bounty. Some men just have a talent for upsetting sailors.

=== p4 ===
Enter the Royal Navy. After England took Jamaica in sixteen fifty-five, ships started handing out rum instead of beer. Half a pint a day, straight. Now picture a few hundred armed men on a wooden ship, each with half a pint of strong rum inside them. Before lunch. In seventeen forty, Admiral Edward Vernon had seen enough. He ordered the rum to be mixed with water, and split into two servings. The sailors hated him for it. Vernon wore a coat made of a rough fabric called grogram, so they called him Old Grog. And the watered-down rum became grog. Later, lemon and lime juice were added, which, almost by accident, helped fight scurvy. That's why Americans started calling British sailors limeys. So the most powerful navy on Earth got its vitamin C from a rum cocktail. In eighteen oh five, Admiral Nelson died winning the battle of Trafalgar. To bring his body home, they sealed it in a barrel of alcohol. Legend says that when the barrel was opened in England, it was nearly empty, because sailors had been secretly sipping from it on the way. That's why rum is still nicknamed Nelson's blood. Historians say it was actually brandy, and probably nobody drank it. But sailors never let the truth ruin a good nickname. And that daily ration? It lasted a very long time. Hold that thought.

=== p5 ===
And pirates? Of course they drank rum. But here's a surprise. "Yo ho ho, and a bottle of rum" is not a real pirate song. It comes from a novel, Treasure Island, written in eighteen eighty-three. That's more than a hundred and fifty years after the golden age of piracy ended. Real pirates drank whatever they could steal. Wine. Brandy. Beer. Rum was simply the cheapest thing in the Caribbean. But one real buccaneer did get famous for rum. Henry Morgan. A Welsh privateer who raided Spanish cities, and then, instead of being hanged, was knighted and made lieutenant governor of Jamaica. He spent his last years drinking. A lot. Today his name is on one of the best-selling rums in the world. Not bad for a pirate.

=== p6 ===
But the biggest rum drinkers weren't sailors or pirates. They were in New England. Ships brought molasses up from the Caribbean, and distilleries in Boston and Rhode Island turned it into rum. By some estimates, the average colonist drank about three gallons of it a year. Rum was money. And here's where the story comes back, as promised. Ships carried that rum to West Africa, where it was traded for enslaved people. They were shipped across the ocean to the Caribbean, to grow more sugar. Which made more molasses. Which made more rum. Historians call it the triangle trade. Real routes were messier than a neat triangle, but the cycle was real, and it ran for more than a century. Every barrel of rum had a human price.

=== p7 ===
Then London got greedy. First, a tax on molasses from the French islands, which colonists simply smuggled around. Then, in seventeen sixty-four, the Sugar Act, and this time Britain actually tried to collect. The colonists were furious. Years later, John Adams wrote: "I know not why we should blush to confess that molasses was an essential ingredient in American independence." Rum was in politics, too. When a young George Washington ran for the Virginia assembly in seventeen fifty-eight, his campaign handed out about a hundred and sixty gallons of rum, punch, wine, beer and cider. To fewer than four hundred voters. He won. And molasses had one more surprise left. On a warm January day in nineteen nineteen, in Boston, a giant tank holding millions of litres of molasses burst open. A wave of sticky black syrup, taller than a house, rolled through the streets faster than a man could run. Twenty-one people died. For years afterwards, locals said the neighbourhood still smelled sweet on hot days. Then, in the nineteen twenties, America banned alcohol completely. So rum came back the way it had always travelled. By sea. Ships loaded with liquor anchored just outside American waters, in a line people called Rum Row. Fast little boats ran it to shore at night. One smuggler, Bill McCoy, was famous for never watering his rum down. Some say that's where "the real McCoy" comes from. It probably isn't. But it's a great story.

=== p8 ===
Meanwhile, Americans who wanted a legal drink simply sailed to Cuba. Havana became a cocktail capital. The daiquiri. The mojito. The Cuba libre. A Cuban company called Bacardi even put a bat on its label, because fruit bats lived in the roof of its first distillery. And after Prohibition ended, a few bar owners in California built fake tropical islands, with bamboo, torches, and drinks with tiny umbrellas. Tiki was born. Kill-devil had become a holiday in a glass. And the Navy's daily ration? It survived for more than three hundred years. Until the thirty-first of July, nineteen seventy. Sailors still call it Black Tot Day. They wore black armbands, and some ships held a funeral for the rum. So, how did trash become the drink of navies, pirates and presidents? It was cheap. It was strong. It never spoiled. And behind every barrel was a sugar empire, built on stolen lives. Something bitter, turned into something sweet. Would you drink it? Tell me in the comments. And next time: the green drink that people said drove artists mad. Absinthe.

## Notes and sources to verify before recording
- Sugar cane domesticated in New Guinea (c. 8000 BC); Columbus brought cane to Hispaniola on the 2nd voyage, 1493.
- Transatlantic slave trade: ~12.5 million embarked; the majority to sugar colonies — "by the millions" is safe.
- Barbados c. 1651: "The chief fuddling they make in the island is Rumbullion, alias Kill-Divil… a hot, hellish, and terrible liquor." Origin of "rumbullion" uncertain (Devon dialect "uproar") — script says "might be".
- Royal Navy: rum ration after capture of Jamaica 1655 (formalised 1731); Vernon's Order 1740 (Old Grog, grogram); citrus added later (lemon juice 1795, limes later); "limey" = US slang. Black Tot Day 31 July 1970 (1655→1970 = 315 years).
- "Yo ho ho" — Stevenson, Treasure Island (1883). Golden Age of Piracy ends c. 1726–1730.
- Henry Morgan: knighted 1674, lieutenant governor of Jamaica; died 1688 after heavy drinking. Captain Morgan brand named after him.
- Colonial consumption: c. 3–4 gallons of rum per person per year (estimates vary) — "about three gallons… by some estimates".
- Triangle trade: simplification acknowledged in script.
- Molasses Act 1733, Sugar Act 1764. John Adams to William Tudor, 1818: "I know not why we should blush to confess that molasses was an essential ingredient in American independence."
- Washington 1758 Frederick County: c. 160 gallons of drink for 391 voters (widely cited from his accounts).
- Rum Rebellion, NSW, 26 Jan 1808: Bligh deposed by NSW Corps officers; rum as de-facto currency and Bligh's moves against the rum trade were a key cause (also land/power disputes).
- Nelson 1805: body preserved in a leaguer of brandy (with spirits of wine) on HMS Victory; "tapping the admiral" / "Nelson's blood" = legend.
- Great Molasses Flood, Boston North End, 15 Jan 1919: ~8.7 million litres (2.3M US gal), wave up to ~7.5 m, ~55 km/h, 21 dead, ~150 injured; tank held molasses for distilling (industrial alcohol). Smell lingering for years = local accounts.
- Bacardi: founded 1862, Santiago de Cuba; fruit bats in the distillery rafters → bat logo.
- Prohibition 1920–1933; Rum Row; Bill McCoy; "real McCoy" attested before him (so "probably isn't").
- Daiquiri, mojito, Cuba libre — Cuba c. 1900–1930s; Tiki: Don the Beachcomber (1933–34), Trader Vic.
- TTS: avoid "often" (use "offen" if needed).
