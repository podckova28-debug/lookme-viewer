# The ENTIRE History of Coffee — script v1

Format: same as absinthe (white background, ink + watercolour, stick-figure "you", handwritten notes), male narrator
(Kokoro bm_fable via Chatterbox, exaggeration 0.5, cfg 0.5, temp 0.7). Hook = scene + paradox + big number + question;
every part ends on a hook. One sentence per line (line index = shot cue in shots.py). Numbers spelled out for TTS.
Target ~7 min, 9 parts (p0…p8). Legends are flagged as legends in the text ("the story goes", "is said to").

=== p0 ===
You are in a coffeehouse in Istanbul.
It's the year sixteen thirty three, and it's late at night.
The room is full of smoke, and talk, and tiny cups of black, bitter coffee.
Then a tall stranger in a plain cloak walks in.
Nobody looks at him twice.
But under that cloak is the Sultan himself, Murad the Fourth.
And he has just made coffee a crime.
The story goes that he walked the streets in disguise, and punished anyone he caught drinking it.
Today, the world drinks around two billion cups of coffee every single day.
So why did kings, sultans and priests try to ban it?
And how did a berry from one forest in Africa end up in your kitchen?
Let's find out.

=== p1 ===
The most famous story starts in Ethiopia, with a goat herder named Kaldi.
One day, his goats ate red berries from a strange bush, and started dancing all night.
It's a lovely story.
But nobody wrote it down until sixteen seventy one, almost a thousand years later.
What we do know is this.
Wild coffee really does come from the forests of Ethiopia.
And the first real evidence of people drinking it comes from across the Red Sea, in Yemen, in the fourteen hundreds.
Sufi monks roasted the beans, boiled them, and drank the dark liquid to stay awake for their night prayers.
They called it qahwa.
A drink to keep you awake for God.
But it didn't stay in the monastery for long.

=== p2 ===
By the early fifteen hundreds, coffee had spread to Mecca, and Cairo.
And a new kind of place appeared.
The coffeehouse.
Men came to drink, play chess, listen to music, and above all, talk.
About poetry.
About prices.
About the people in charge.
And that was the problem.
In fifteen eleven, the governor of Mecca, Khair Beg, banned coffee.
Sacks of beans were burned in the streets.
But the Sultan in Cairo loved coffee, and the ban was soon overturned.
In fifteen fifty five, two men from Syria opened the first coffeehouse in Istanbul.
People called these places schools of the wise.
Rulers called them something else.
And for the next hundred years, sultans banned them, and opened them, and banned them again.
Coffee was too popular to stop.
So the next question was, who would get rich from it?

=== p3 ===
For two hundred years, almost all the world's coffee came from one country.
Yemen.
It left through a port called Mocha.
Yes, that Mocha.
To protect their monopoly, traders made sure no fertile seed ever left the country.
The beans were roasted or boiled first, so they could never grow.
But around sixteen seventy, a pilgrim named Baba Budan is said to have hidden seven green seeds in his clothes on his way home from Mecca.
He planted them in the hills of southern India.
Seven seeds.
The monopoly was cracked.
And at the same moment, coffee was reaching a new and very suspicious market.
Europe.

=== p4 ===
Venetian merchants brought coffee to Europe in the early sixteen hundreds.
Some priests called it the bitter invention of Satan.
The story goes that they asked Pope Clement the Eighth to ban it.
He tasted a cup, and said it was so good, it would be a sin to let only unbelievers drink it.
That story is probably invented.
But the coffee was real, and it was spreading fast.
In sixteen fifty two, a man named Pasqua Rosée opened the first coffeehouse in London.
Fifty years later, there were hundreds.
For one penny, you got a cup of coffee and the right to join any conversation in the room.
People called them penny universities.
Scientists met there.
Traders met there.
One coffeehouse, owned by Edward Lloyd, was where ship owners and merchants came to insure their ships.
It became Lloyd's of London, one of the biggest insurance markets in the world.
Coffee was changing how Europe did business.
And not everyone was happy about it.

=== p5 ===
In sixteen seventy four, a pamphlet appeared in London.
The Women's Petition Against Coffee.
It complained that men spent all day in coffeehouses, and came home useless.
The next year, King Charles the Second went further.
He banned coffeehouses completely.
He was afraid they were places where people plotted against him.
The ban lasted eleven days.
The public was furious, and the king backed down.
In Paris, the Café Procope opened in sixteen eighty six.
The writer Voltaire is said to have drunk forty or fifty cups a day.
In Leipzig, the composer Bach even wrote a funny little opera about a girl who refuses to give up coffee.
And in Prussia, King Frederick the Great told his people to drink beer instead.
He even hired former soldiers to sniff the streets for the smell of illegal roasting.
They were called the coffee sniffers.
Europe was hooked.
But someone had to grow all that coffee.

=== p6 ===
This is the dark part of the story.
The Dutch planted coffee on the island of Java, in what is now Indonesia.
And in seventeen fourteen, they gave a coffee plant to King Louis the Fourteenth of France.
A few years later, a French naval officer named Gabriel de Clieu carried a seedling from Paris across the Atlantic.
The story goes that when the drinking water ran low, he shared his own ration with the plant.
It reached the island of Martinique, in the Caribbean.
From there, coffee spread to French colonies.
By the seventeen eighties, the colony of Saint Domingue, today's Haiti, grew about half of the world's coffee.
It was grown by hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans, in brutal conditions.
In seventeen ninety one, they rose up, and won.
Haiti became the first free Black republic.
Then coffee moved to Brazil.
And Brazil grew it with slavery too, until slavery there finally ended, in eighteen eighty eight.
By then, Brazil was growing more coffee than anyone on Earth.
It still does.
Now, the drink was cheap enough for everyone.
Including armies.

=== p7 ===
After the Boston Tea Party in seventeen seventy three, drinking tea in America started to feel unpatriotic.
Coffee took its place.
In the American Civil War, every Union soldier was given about thirty six pounds of coffee a year.
One rifle even had a small coffee grinder built into its stock.
Soldiers wrote about coffee in their diaries more than about bullets.
In nineteen oh one, in Milan, an engineer named Luigi Bezzera patented a machine that forced steam through coffee, at speed.
Espresso.
Fast coffee.
In nineteen thirty eight, Nestlé launched instant coffee, Nescafé.
And in the Second World War, American soldiers carried it all over the world.
Coffee wasn't a luxury anymore.
It was fuel.
So what is it actually doing to your brain?

=== p8 ===
During the day, your brain builds up a chemical called adenosine.
It makes you feel tired.
Caffeine looks a lot like adenosine, so it sits in the same places in your brain, and blocks it.
You still need sleep.
You just can't feel it yet.
Today, coffee is one of the most traded farm products on Earth.
Around two billion cups a day.
From the monks of Yemen to the coffee shop on your street.
So why did rulers keep trying to ban it?
Maybe it was never really about the drink.
It was about the places where people drank it.
Places where anyone could sit down, wake up, and start asking questions.
Would you have risked the Sultan's sword for a cup of coffee?
Tell me in the comments.
And next time: gin.
The drink that nearly destroyed London.

## Notes and sources to verify
- Murad IV (r. 1623–1640) banned coffee, tobacco and alcohol c. 1633; night patrols in disguise and executions are reported by later
  chroniclers (often exaggerated) → told as "the story goes".
- ~2–2.25 billion cups/day (commonly cited; International Coffee Organization-based estimates).
- Kaldi legend first written by Antoine Faustus Nairon, 1671 (De saluberrima potione cahue).
- Coffea arabica native to SW Ethiopia highlands. Earliest credible evidence: Sufi shrines in Yemen, mid-15th c. (al-Dhabhani).
- Mecca ban 1511 (Khair Beg), overturned by the Mamluk sultan in Cairo.
- First Istanbul coffeehouse 1554/1555 by Hakam (Aleppo) and Shams (Damascus); "mekteb-i irfan" (schools of the wise).
- Mocha (al-Makha) port; boiling/roasting of exported beans (traditional claim). Baba Budan, 7 seeds, Chikmagalur, c. 1670 (legend).
- Coffee in Venice from c. 1615; Pope Clement VIII "baptism" story is apocryphal (Clement died 1605) — script says "probably invented".
- Pasqua Rosée, St Michael's Alley, Cornhill, 1652. Oxford had the first English coffeehouse, 1650 (Jacob) — not mentioned.
- Penny universities; Edward Lloyd's coffeehouse c. 1688 → Lloyd's of London.
- Women's Petition Against Coffee, 1674. Charles II proclamation 29 Dec 1675, withdrawn 8 Jan 1676 (≈11 days).
- Café Procope 1686; Voltaire 40–50 cups — anecdote. Bach, Coffee Cantata BWV 211 (c. 1734).
- Frederick the Great, coffee manifesto 1777; "Kaffeeriecher" (coffee sniffers) from 1781, mostly disabled soldiers.
- Dutch Java plantations from 1696/1699; plant to Louis XIV 1714 (Jardin des Plantes); de Clieu to Martinique 1720s (water ration legend).
- Saint-Domingue ~half of world coffee late 1780s; revolution from 1791, independence 1804 (Haiti).
- Brazil: coffee from 1727 (Palheta), largest producer from c. 1840; abolition 13 May 1888 (Lei Áurea).
- Union ration ~36 lb coffee/yr per soldier (Hughes, "Coffee in the Civil War"); Sharps carbine with grinder in buttstock (c. 100 made).
- Luigi Bezzera patent 1901 (Milan); Nescafé 1938 (Switzerland), widely used by US forces in WWII.
- Caffeine = adenosine receptor antagonist.
