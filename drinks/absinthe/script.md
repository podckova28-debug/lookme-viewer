# The ENTIRE History of Absinthe — script v1

Format: same as mead and rum (white background, ink + watercolour, stick-figure "you", handwritten notes), male narrator (Kokoro bm_fable via Chatterbox).
New rules from the breakout-video analysis (history/ages_pilot/README.md): the hook = scene + paradox + big number + open question;
every part ends on a question or a "but"; numbers and dates go into the handwritten notes on screen.
Target ~7.5 min. 9 parts (p0…p8). The murders in p6 are told plainly, no jokes, no gore.

=== p0 ===
You are in a café in Paris. It's the year eighteen ninety, and it's five o'clock. The green hour. A waiter puts down a glass of bright green liquid, a flat spoon full of holes, and a single cube of sugar. You pour ice cold water over the sugar, drop by drop. And the green drink turns cloudy, like milk. Around you, half of Paris is doing the same thing. France drinks thirty six million litres of this a year. And within twenty five years, it will be banned in almost every country in Europe, and in America. Accused of making people mad, blind, and violent. So what was in that glass? And why did the whole world turn against it? Almost everything people believed about absinthe was wrong. Let's find out how wrong.

=== p1 ===
The story starts in a quiet valley in the Swiss mountains, called the Val de Travers. At the end of the seventeen hundreds, people there made a bitter medicine from a plant called wormwood. Wormwood had been used against worms, fevers and stomach pain since ancient Egypt. The legend says a French doctor named Pierre Ordinaire mixed it with anise and fennel, soaked it in strong alcohol, and sold it as a cure for almost everything. Whether he invented it, or simply copied two local sisters, nobody really knows. But in seventeen ninety seven, a businessman bought the recipe. His son in law, Henri Louis Pernod, built a distillery. And in eighteen oh five, he moved it across the border to France, to a town called Pontarlier. A medicine was about to become a habit. But first, it needed an army.

=== p2 ===
In the eighteen forties, French soldiers were fighting in Algeria. The water was dirty, and fever was everywhere. So the army gave them absinthe to mix into their water. Did it work against disease? Not really. But the soldiers came home with a new favourite drink. And they brought the ritual with them. Here's how it works. Absinthe is very strong, up to seventy percent alcohol. So you never drink it straight. You put a sugar cube on a slotted spoon, and drip cold water through it. Three to five parts water to one part absinthe. As the water hits, the oils from anise and fennel can't stay dissolved, and they turn into millions of tiny droplets. That's why the drink turns cloudy. The French call it the louche. It looks like magic. It's actually chemistry. And soon, the whole country wanted to watch it happen.

=== p3 ===
Then, a tiny bug arrived from America. A pest called phylloxera, smaller than a grain of sand. It ate the roots of grapevines. Starting in the eighteen sixties, it destroyed most of the vineyards in France. Wine became rare and expensive. And absinthe, made from cheap alcohol and herbs, became the drink of everyone. Workers. Soldiers. Bankers. Even women, who were not supposed to drink in public. In eighteen seventy four, France drank about seven hundred thousand litres of absinthe. By nineteen ten, thirty six million. Cafés filled up every evening at five, for the green hour. There was so much of it that in nineteen oh one, when lightning set the Pernod factory on fire, workers opened the tanks and poured a million litres of absinthe into the river, so the fire wouldn't spread. Two days later, a spring twenty kilometres away started to smell of anise, and turned cloudy. That's how scientists proved the two rivers were connected underground. Absinthe was everywhere. Even in the rivers. And now it was about to get a nickname.

=== p4 ===
They called it the green fairy. In French: la fée verte. And the artists of Paris fell in love with her. The poets Verlaine and Rimbaud drank it while they wrote. The painter Toulouse Lautrec is said to have carried absinthe inside a hollow walking cane, so he never had to go without. Edgar Degas painted a woman staring into a glass of it, and critics called the painting a disgrace. Van Gogh painted a still life with a glass of absinthe on a café table. And Oscar Wilde supposedly said: after the first glass, you see things as you wish they were. After the second, you see things as they are not. Finally, you see things as they really are, and that is the most horrible thing in the world. To artists, the green fairy was inspiration. To their enemies, she was something much darker.

=== p5 ===
In the eighteen sixties, a French doctor named Valentin Magnan gave pure wormwood oil to guinea pigs and dogs. The animals had seizures. Magnan announced a new disease. Absinthism. Worse than ordinary drunkenness, he said. Hallucinations. Madness. Crime. The suspect was a chemical in wormwood called thujone. But here's what Magnan didn't say. His animals got huge doses of pure oil. Nobody drinks that. A drinker would die from the alcohol long before the thujone could do anything. The real danger was somewhere else. Cheap brands made from industrial alcohol, and coloured green with copper salts, which actually are poisonous. And the people shouting loudest about absinthe weren't always doctors. Wine makers had recovered from the phylloxera, and they wanted their customers back. The anti alcohol movement needed a villain. All they were missing was a crime.

=== p6 ===
On the twenty eighth of August, nineteen oh five, in a village in Switzerland, a farm worker named Jean Lanfray shot his pregnant wife and his two little daughters. Then he tried to kill himself, and failed. That day, he had drunk wine, brandy, cognac, crème de menthe, and two glasses of absinthe. But the newspapers only wrote about one of them. The absinthe murders. Within weeks, more than eighty thousand people signed a petition to ban it. Belgium banned absinthe that same year. Switzerland held a national vote in nineteen oh eight, and wrote the ban into its constitution. The Netherlands followed. Then the United States, in nineteen twelve. And in nineteen fifteen, in the middle of the First World War, France banned its own national drink. Thirty six million litres a year, gone. Almost overnight. But did it really disappear?

=== p7 ===
Not quite. Pernod and the other makers simply took out the wormwood, kept the anise, and made a new drink. Pastis. Same cloudy louche, no green fairy. And back in the valley where it all began, the Val de Travers, people kept making absinthe in secret. For ninety five years. They made it clear instead of green, so it would look like water to the police. Locals called it La Bleue. The police knew. Half the village knew. And the bottles kept coming. Meanwhile, the green fairy became a legend. A forbidden drink that made you see things. Which was exactly the kind of story that would bring her back.

=== p8 ===
In the nineteen nineties, bars in Britain found out absinthe had never actually been banned there. It came back from the Czech Republic, in bright green bottles. Scientists finally tested real bottles from before the ban. The thujone was far too low to cause hallucinations. The green fairy had been mostly alcohol and myth. The European Union allowed it again. Switzerland made it legal in two thousand five. The United States, in two thousand seven. And France officially cancelled its ban in two thousand eleven. So, why did the world turn against absinthe? It was strong. It was everywhere. It had powerful enemies. And one terrible crime gave them the story they needed. The drink that was banned for making people mad was mostly guilty of being too popular. Would you try it? Tell me in the comments. And next time: coffee. The drink that kings and sultans tried to ban.
