=== p0 ===
You are walking through London.
It's seventeen thirty six, and you're in a poor district called St Giles.
Over a cellar door hangs a hand painted sign.
Drunk for a penny.
Dead drunk for two pence.
Clean straw for nothing.
The straw, by the way, is for lying on, when your legs stop working.
Customer service.
In this street, one house in four sells the same drink.
Gin.
Parliament will try to stop it.
Again.
And again.
And again.
Spoiler: gin wins most of the rounds.
And yet today, gin is the drink of garden parties and royalty.
So how did the drink that nearly destroyed London end up in a glass with ice and a slice of lime?

=== p1 ===
It starts not in England, but in Holland.
Dutch doctors soaked juniper berries in alcohol, as medicine.
Juniper was supposed to help with stomach pain, kidney stones, even the plague.
It did not cure the plague.
But it tasted a lot better than most medicine.
So patients kept asking for more medicine.
Funny how that works.
The Dutch called it jeneever, after their word for juniper.
In the sixteen hundreds, English soldiers fought alongside the Dutch against Spain.
Before a battle, the Dutch troops took a swig of jeneever.
Then they charged.
The English watched, very impressed.
And gave it a name: Dutch courage.
The soldiers brought the taste home.
But it took a Dutch king to turn it into a disaster.

=== p2 ===
In sixteen eighty eight, a Dutch prince, William of Orange, became King of England.
He was at war with France.
So he put heavy taxes on French brandy.
And he made it legal for almost anyone to distil spirits from English grain.
No licence.
No training.
No idea what they were doing.
Farmers loved it.
Landowners loved it.
Parliament loved it.
Soon there were stills in kitchens, sheds and back rooms all over London.
The English couldn't say jeneever, so they shortened it.
Gin.
Cheaper than beer, stronger than wine, and sold by anyone, to anyone.
What could possibly go wrong?

=== p3 ===
Everything.
By the seventeen twenties, London was in the grip of the Gin Craze.
Remember the coffeehouses from our coffee episode, where merchants talked business over a cup?
A few streets away, a penny bought something very different.
There were thousands of gin shops in London.
Gin was sold from barrows, from cellars, from barber shops.
Even from prisons.
Some of it was flavoured with turpentine.
Yes, the paint thinner.
Workers were sometimes paid in gin.
People started calling it Mother's Ruin.
In some years, more people were buried in London than were born there.
Parliament tried a tax.
Score: Gin, one. Parliament, nil.
And then came a crime that shocked the whole city.

=== p4 ===
In seventeen thirty four, a woman named Judith Defoor collected her two year old daughter from a workhouse.
The child had been given new clothes there.
Judith took the little girl away, and killed her.
Then she sold the clothes for one shilling and four pence, and spent the money on gin.
She was tried, found guilty, and hanged.
Newspapers and preachers made her the face of the Gin Craze.
For many Londoners, gin was no longer a joke.
And Parliament was ready to try again.

=== p5 ===
In seventeen thirty six, Parliament passed the Gin Act.
To sell gin, you now needed a licence that cost fifty pounds.
A fortune.
So, how many people bought one?
Two.
In the whole country.
Everyone else kept selling, and simply changed the name.
Ladies' Delight.
Cuckold's Comfort.
And, our favourite, Parliament Gin.
One seller, Dudley Bradstreet, painted a cat on his window.
You whispered puss, put a coin in the cat's mouth, and gin poured out of a pipe under its paw.
Some call it one of the first vending machines.
The government paid informers to report the sellers.
Angry crowds chased the informers through the streets.
Score: Gin, two. Parliament, still nil.
Just like absinthe in Paris, a century and a half later, gin was blamed for everything.
And it took a painter to change people's minds.

=== p6 ===
In seventeen fifty one, the artist William Hogarth published two prints.
The first was Beer Street.
Healthy, happy workers, drinking beer, building things.
The only man going out of business is the pawnbroker.
The second was Gin Lane.
Houses falling down.
A pawnshop doing great business.
And a drunk mother letting her baby fall over a railing.
Subtle, it was not.
But everyone got the message.
That same year, Parliament passed a smarter law, with proper licences for the shops.
Then grain prices went up, and cheap gin wasn't cheap anymore.
Slowly, the craze faded.
Score: Parliament, finally, one.
But gin wasn't finished.
It just needed a makeover.

=== p7 ===
In the eighteen thirties, a new kind of still made gin cleaner and lighter.
London Dry.
No sugar, no turpentine, just juniper and spices.
Gin palaces opened, with gas lights, mirrors and polished brass.
Same drink, much better lighting.
Meanwhile, British officers in India were fighting malaria.
The medicine was quinine, and it was horribly bitter.
So they mixed it with soda water, sugar and lime.
And then, purely for medical reasons, they added gin.
The gin and tonic.
Remember the Dutch doctors? Gin was medicine again.
Sort of.
The story goes that the Royal Navy tested its gin by pouring it on gunpowder and lighting it.
If the powder still burned, the gin was strong enough.
That's Navy Strength.
And when America banned alcohol in nineteen twenty, gin was easy to fake in a bathtub.
Bathtub gin tasted awful.
So bartenders hid it in cocktails.
The martini.
The Tom Collins.
From the gutter, to the cocktail bar.

=== p8 ===
Today, there are hundreds of gin distilleries in Britain alone.
The Queen Mother was said to enjoy a gin and Dubonnet before lunch.
The drink that once sold for a penny in St Giles now sells for forty pounds a bottle.
Straw not included.
So, did gin nearly destroy London?
It wasn't really the juniper.
It was cheap alcohol, sold with no rules, to people with nothing else.
When the rules changed, the drink changed too.
Final score?
Parliament lost almost every round, but London survived, and gin got a glass with ice and lime.
Would you have drunk a penny's worth in Gin Lane?
Tell me in the comments.
And next time: a drink that started out as actual trash, and ended up ruling the navy and the pirates.
Rum.
