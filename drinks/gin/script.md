# The Entire History of GIN — script v1

Format: same as coffee/absinthe (white background, ink + watercolour, stick-figure "you", handwritten notes), male narrator
(Kokoro bm_fable via Chatterbox, exaggeration 0.5, cfg 0.5, temp 0.7). Hook = scene + paradox + big number + question;
every part ends on a hook. One sentence per line (line index = shot cue in shots.py). Numbers spelled out for TTS.
Cross-links: p3 mentions the coffee episode (London coffeehouses), p5 mentions absinthe; ending teases rum.
The Judith Defour case (p4) is told plainly, without detail. Target ~7 min, 9 parts.

=== p0 ===
You are walking through London.
It's the year seventeen thirty six, in a poor district called St Giles.
Over a doorway hangs a hand painted sign.
Drunk for a penny.
Dead drunk for two pence.
Clean straw for nothing.
The straw is for lying on, when you can't stand up anymore.
In this street, one house in four sells the same drink.
Gin.
Across England, people are drinking millions of gallons of it every year.
Parliament will try to stop it again and again.
And yet today, gin is the drink of garden parties and royalty.
So how did the drink that nearly destroyed London end up in a glass with ice and a slice of lime?

=== p1 ===
The story starts not in England, but in Holland.
For centuries, doctors there had soaked juniper berries in alcohol.
Juniper was supposed to help with stomach pain, kidney stones, even the plague.
The Dutch called the drink jeneever, after their word for juniper.
It was strong, it was cheap, and it tasted a lot better than most medicine.
A legend says a Dutch professor named Franciscus Sylvius invented it in the sixteen fifties.
But juniper spirits were being sold in Holland a hundred years before he was born.
In the sixteen hundreds, English soldiers fought alongside the Dutch in their long wars against Spain.
Before a battle, the Dutch troops took a swig of jeneever.
The English watched them charge, and gave it a name.
Dutch courage.
The soldiers brought the taste home.
But it took a Dutch king to make it a disaster.

=== p2 ===
In sixteen eighty eight, a Dutch prince, William of Orange, became King of England.
He was at war with France.
So he did two things.
He put heavy taxes on French brandy.
And he made it legal for almost anyone to distil spirits from English grain, with no licence at all.
Farmers loved it, because it gave them a market for bad barley.
Landowners loved it.
Parliament loved it.
Within a few years, there were stills in kitchens, sheds and back rooms all over London.
The English couldn't say jeneever, so they shortened it.
Gin.
Cheaper than beer.
Stronger than wine.
And sold by anyone, to anyone.
What could possibly go wrong?

=== p3 ===
By the seventeen twenties, London was in the grip of what we now call the Gin Craze.
Remember the coffeehouses from our coffee episode?
The penny universities, where merchants talked business and scientists talked ideas?
A few streets away, in the poorer parts of town, a penny bought something very different.
There were thousands of gin shops in London.
Gin was sold from barrows, from cellars, from barber shops and prisons.
Some of it was flavoured with turpentine.
Some was strengthened with sulphuric acid.
Workers were paid in gin.
Mothers gave it to crying babies to keep them quiet.
People called it Mother's Ruin.
In some years, more people were buried in London than were born there.
And then came a crime that shocked the whole city.

=== p4 ===
In seventeen thirty four, a woman named Judith Defoor collected her two year old daughter from a workhouse.
The child had been given new clothes there.
Judith took the little girl away, and killed her.
Then she sold the clothes, for one shilling and four pence.
And spent the money on gin.
She was tried, found guilty, and hanged.
Newspapers and preachers made her the face of the Gin Craze.
For many Londoners, gin was no longer a joke.
It was a plague.
And Parliament was finally ready to act.
The question was, would anyone obey?

=== p5 ===
Just like absinthe in France a century and a half later, gin was blamed for madness, crime and the ruin of the nation.
In seventeen thirty six, Parliament passed the Gin Act.
To sell gin, you now needed a licence that cost fifty pounds.
A fortune.
Only two people in the whole country ever paid it.
Everyone else simply kept selling, in secret.
Shops renamed gin.
They called it Cuckold's Comfort, Ladies' Delight, and Parliament Gin.
One clever seller, Dudley Bradstreet, painted a cat on his window.
You whispered puss, put a coin in the cat's mouth, and gin poured out through a pipe under its paw.
Some call it one of the first vending machines.
The government paid informers to report illegal sellers.
Angry crowds hunted the informers through the streets.
The law had failed.
And it took a painter to change people's minds.

=== p6 ===
In seventeen fifty one, the artist William Hogarth published two prints, side by side.
The first was called Beer Street.
Happy, healthy workers, drinking beer, building things.
The second was called Gin Lane.
A broken staircase.
A pawnshop doing great business.
A drunk mother letting her baby fall over a railing.
Everyone understood it.
That same year, Parliament passed a new law, and this time it was smarter.
Gin shops needed proper licences.
Distillers could only sell to licensed shops.
And at the same time, the price of grain went up.
Slowly, the craze faded.
But gin itself didn't go anywhere.
It just needed a new image.

=== p7 ===
In the eighteen thirties, a new kind of still made gin cleaner and lighter.
This was London Dry gin, no sugar, no turpentine, just juniper and spices.
Huge gin palaces opened, with gas lights, mirrors and polished brass.
Meanwhile, British officers in India were fighting another enemy.
Malaria.
The medicine was quinine, from the bark of a tree, and it was horribly bitter.
So they mixed it with soda water, sugar and lime.
And then, they added gin.
The gin and tonic.
A drink that was, at least in theory, good for you.
The Royal Navy carried gin too.
Sailors tested it by pouring it on gunpowder and lighting it.
If the powder still burned, the gin was strong enough.
That's where the name Navy Strength comes from.
And when America banned alcohol in nineteen twenty, gin was easy to fake in a bathtub.
Cocktails were invented partly to hide the taste.
The martini.
The Tom Collins.
Gin had gone from the gutter to the cocktail bar.

=== p8 ===
Today, there are hundreds of gin distilleries in Britain alone.
The Queen Mother was said to enjoy a gin and Dubonnet before lunch, almost every day.
The same drink that once sold for a penny in St Giles now sells for forty pounds a bottle.
So did gin nearly destroy London?
It wasn't really the juniper.
It was cheap alcohol, sold with no rules, to people with nothing else.
When the rules changed, the drink changed too.
From medicine, to Dutch courage, to Mother's Ruin, to the gin and tonic.
Would you have drunk a penny's worth in Gin Lane?
Tell me in the comments.
And if you want to know about a drink that started out as actual trash, and ended up ruling the navy and the pirates, watch our episode on rum.

## Notes and sources to verify
- St Giles sign "Drunk for a penny, dead drunk for twopence, clean straw for nothing" — quoted by Smollett (History of England); used here as the classic sign.
- "One house in four" in St Giles sold gin (c. 1750 reports); 7,000+ gin shops in London (1730s estimates); England & Wales c. 8 million gallons/yr by 1743.
- Burials exceeded baptisms in London for much of 1720s–1740s (Bills of Mortality).
- Genever: juniper spirits in Dutch medical texts from the 16th c.; "Dutch courage" — Eighty Years' War / Thirty Years' War era.
- 1690 Act for the Encouraging of the Distilling of Brandy and Spirits from Corn; duties on French imports under William III.
- Judith Defour, Old Bailey, March 1734 (daughter Mary, clothes sold for 1s 4d), hanged.
- Gin Act 1736: £50 retail licence; only two licences taken out; riots; informers attacked. Mock names ("Cuckold's Comfort", "Ladies' Delight", "Parliament Gin" / "Parliament Brandy").
- Dudley Bradstreet "Puss and Mew" (c. 1736–38), described in his memoirs.
- Hogarth, Beer Street and Gin Lane (Feb 1751); Gin Act 1751 (Tippling Act); grain prices rose in late 1750s.
- Aeneas Coffey column still patent 1830 → London Dry style.
- Quinine tonic in British India, 19th c.; gin and tonic.
- US Prohibition 1920–1933, "bathtub gin"; cocktails partly masked bad spirits.
- Sources: Jessica Warner, Craze: Gin and Debauchery in an Age of Reason (2002); Patrick Dillon, Gin: The Much-Lamented Death of Madam Geneva (2002); Richard Barnett, The Dedalus Book of Gin (2011); Old Bailey Proceedings, t17340227-32.
