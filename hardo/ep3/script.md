# Why It's So Hard to Be a Teacher — Episode 3 (~6 min, 30 blocks)

Style: Dinzo. White background, flat vector, second person, deadpan. Narrator: Grady (ElevenLabs), tempo x1.2.

## COLD OPEN
1. Ten fifty-two p.m. Your kitchen table. Notebook one hundred and seventeen out of one hundred and fifty. The red pen is dying. The essay is called "My Summer," and the kid wrote one sentence.
2. You read the sentence twice. You put the pen down. Somewhere in the house a dishwasher finishes, and that is the most exciting thing that will happen to you tonight.
## REWIND
3. You've been awake since five forty. To understand how you ended up here, with notebook one seventeen, rewind to this morning, when you still believed in the lesson plan.
## CHAPTER 1 — MORNING
4. Six forty. The school is empty and the copier is already jammed. The copier has moods. Today's mood is "paper tray two." You fix it with a ruler and a word you'll never say in class.
5. Seven thirty. First bell. Twenty-eight kids. Three absent, one asleep, one who did the homework for a different subject, and one who is quietly the smartest person in the building and does not know it yet.
6. You take attendance. You collect phones. You collect the phones again, because the first collection was a decoy. You smile. The smile is part of the uniform.
7. Period one is fractions. You explain fractions. You explain fractions a second way, a third way, with pizza, with money, with a song you regret immediately. On the fourth way, it lands. For four kids.
8. A hand goes up. "Will this be on the test?" You say yes. It will not be on the test. But now, for the next forty minutes, it is the most important thing in the room. That's the trick.
## CHAPTER 2 — THE DAY
9. Period three. Fire drill. Twenty-eight kids, one door, zero sense of urgency. You count heads in the parking lot. Twenty-seven. Then twenty-eight. The last one went back for his snack.
10. Period four. A kid asks a question you don't know the answer to. You say "great question, let's find out together." This is teacher for "I will Google this tonight at eleven."
11. Lunch is twenty minutes. The line takes thirteen. You eat the remaining seven standing next to the copier, which is jammed again, as a kind of companionship.
12. You pass the staff bathroom four times before you go in. If this sounds familiar, you've met the nurse from episode one. Same door, different building, same bladder.
13. Your phone shows an email from a parent. Subject: "Quick question." The email is six hundred words long. The question is in paragraph four. You answer it at eleven p.m., in two sentences.
14. Period six is the one after lunch, which means twenty-eight kids powered entirely by sugar and grudges. You teach anyway. Nobody films this part. It would not go viral.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE SUPPLIES
15. You run out of whiteboard markers. The school gave you a budget in September. The budget was gone by October. The markers come from your own wallet, like the stickers, the tissues, and the extra pencils.
16. On average, a teacher in the US spends close to nine hundred dollars a year of their own money on the classroom. Nobody signs a receipt for that. The kids think the markers just appear.
17. You keep a drawer of granola bars. Not for you. For the kid who didn't eat breakfast, who will not say so, and who gets very quiet around ten a.m. You've learned to read quiet.
## CHAPTER 4 — AFTER THE BELL
18. Three fifteen. The bell. The kids leave in eleven seconds. You do not leave. There's bus duty, which is standing in the wind pointing at buses.
19. Four p.m. A meeting about the next meeting. There are slides. There is a word on the slides that was invented last year and will be retired next year. You nod at the word.
20. Five thirty. You finally sit at your desk. It is the first time you've sat today. Your knees file a complaint. You open your laptop, and the real job begins: the one nobody sees.
21. Here's the math from the OECD: a full-time teacher works about forty-one hours a week. Only about twenty-three of those are actual teaching. The rest is planning, grading, emails, and meetings about meetings.
22. Seven p.m. You drive home with a tote bag of notebooks on the passenger seat. It has a seatbelt on. You're not sure when that started. You're not sure it's a joke anymore.
## CHAPTER 5 — THE EVENING
23. Dinner is standing up. Then the table, the pen, the stack. One hundred and fifty notebooks, two minutes each, is five hours. You have three. You do the math on a sticky note and stop doing the math.
24. Somewhere around notebook sixty, the handwriting starts to blur together, and you catch yourself writing "good job" on a page that says nothing. You cross it out. You write a real comment. It takes longer.
25. Your rating today was a kid saying "this was actually okay" on the way out. You will remember it for a week. You will remember the other comment, the one from Tuesday, for a year.
## CHAPTER 6 — THE NUMBERS
26. One widely cited estimate says forty-four percent of new teachers leave within five years. A federal study that followed one group found closer to seventeen. The honest answer is: a lot, and it depends who's counting.
27. The ones who stay don't stay for the money, the hours, or the copier. They stay for the kid in the back row, the one who doesn't know yet. You've already picked yours this year.
## END
28. Eleven thirty. Notebook one seventeen. "My Summer." One sentence: "My summer was good. I was with my grandma. She is gone now." You pick up the red pen. You put it down. You pick up a sticker.
29. Eleven forty. Notebook one fifty. You close it. The dishwasher is quiet. Tomorrow the alarm says five forty, and the copier will be waiting, and so will twenty-eight kids, one of them still asleep.
30. That was one day. Next time: a different job, a different door. Subscribe if you want to be there. Bring your own markers.

Stats used (3):
- Working hours: OECD TALIS 2024, full-time teachers ~41 h/week, ~22.7 h teaching. https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/10/results-from-talis-2024_28fbde1d/full-report/thriving-in-teaching_340c1305.html
- Own money: AdoptAClassroom 2025 Teacher Spending Survey, $895 average in 2024–25. https://www.adoptaclassroom.org/2025/06/09/2025-teacher-survey-spending-stats-classroom-needs/
