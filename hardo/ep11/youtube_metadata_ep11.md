# Episode 11 — YouTube metadata

## Title
The Weirdest Jobs in the World (They're All Real)

## Description
Somewhere right now, a person is sniffing an armpit. For money. On purpose. And they're not the strangest worker on this list.

Ten real jobs that sound made up, counted down from ten to one, plus a bonus that's older than all of them: the bicycle fisher of Amsterdam, the professional line stander, the fortune cookie writer, the water slide tester, the golf ball diver, the odor judge, the snake milker, the ice cream taster with a gold spoon, the chick sexer and Tokyo's white-gloved train pushers.

Every worker in this video is a fictional illustration; the jobs and the numbers are real.

Sources:
• 12,000–15,000 bicycles a year recovered from Amsterdam's canals — Guinness World Records; NPR (2022); PRI The World (2015)
• Line standers in Washington: clients have paid about $40–60 an hour — Sunlight Foundation (2011), Washington Express, Roll Call (2009)
• About 4 million fortune cookies a day; a fortune writer of about 30 years stepped back citing writer's block — NPR (17 Feb 2017), NBC News, Money
• Water slide tester: six months, Egypt, Majorca and Turkey, £20,000 a year pro rata, 30-second video applications — First Choice (2013) via Travel Weekly and NPR
• Golf ball divers: murky water worked by feel, 8–10 hours a day, fishing line and alligators, paid per ball — U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Career Outlook (2014)
• Odor judges rate underarm odor, often on a 1–10 scale, after a deodorant-free week — Princeton Consumer Research, Hill Top Research
• Snake milking: about 150 snakes in two hours, 600–1,000 a week, venom for research — Kentucky Reptile Zoo (Freethink, WDRB, WEKU)
• An ice cream taster's taste buds insured for $1 million; gold-plated spoon, spits it out, avoids garlic, onions and spicy food — CBS News, Los Angeles Times
• Chick sexing: vent sexing developed in Japan in the 1920s–30s; about 800–1,400 chicks an hour at around 99% accuracy — Poultry World (2010), Taipei Times (2010)
• Tokyo rush-hour trains averaged 221% of capacity in 1975; the first pushers at Shinjuku were mostly part-time students — Wikipedia "Passenger pusher" (citing LIFE 1964 and The New York Times 1995)
• Professional mourners: ancient Egypt, Victorian "mutes"; a UK service at about £45 for two hours — Funeral Zone, HuffPost UK (2013)
