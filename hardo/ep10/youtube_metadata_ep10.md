## Title
Why It's So Hard to Be the President

## Description
It's 5:45 in the morning. There's a man on the lawn who has been standing there all night. He's not guarding a person. He's guarding the President. That's you.

One day as a fictional President of the United States, lived from the inside: the morning intelligence folder, a schedule cut into fifteen-minute blocks, the aide with the satchel who is never more than a few steps away, a desk where only the hard problems arrive, tens of thousands of letters a day, a car you're not allowed to drive, two hundred handshakes, what the job does to the body, the pay, the grocery bill, forty minutes of dinner, and the phone at 10 p.m.

A fictional, non-political character. No real president is portrayed.

Based on real sources:
• Salary: $400,000 a year plus a $50,000 expense allowance — 3 U.S.C. §102
• The President's Daily Brief has been presented in some form to presidents since 1946 — Office of the Director of National Intelligence
• The military aide's satchel weighs about 45 lb and is never more than a few steps away, elevators included — Atomic Heritage Foundation / National Museum of Nuclear Science & History
• The White House: 132 rooms, 35 bathrooms, 412 doors, 147 windows; the kitchen can serve dinner to 140 guests — whitehouse.gov, "About the White House"
• Tens of thousands of letters, faxes and emails arrive every day — Office of Presidential Correspondence, White House blog (2009, National Archives)
• Elected heads of government lived about 2.7 years less than the runners-up they beat (17 countries, 1722–2015) — Olenski, Abola & Jena, BMJ 2015;351:h6424
• Official records belong to the public — Presidential Records Act of 1978, National Archives
Long-standing practice, not law: presidents don't drive on public roads; the family pays for its own food, billed monthly. The 5:45 wake-up, the 11-minute lunch and the 200 handshakes are the hero's fictional day.
