# Why It's So Hard to Be the President — Episode 10 (~7.5 min, 30 blocks)

Style: STYLE.md (cook look: thin line, realistic proportions, soft flat colour, light partial setting fading into white). Narrator: Grady (ElevenLabs), tempo x1.1.
Hero: a fictional President of the United States, East Asian American man in his late fifties, short silver-grey hair neatly combed, round tortoiseshell glasses, clean-shaven, charcoal-grey suit, white shirt, plain navy tie, a small plain gold star pin on the lapel, black shoes; a grey cardigan over pyjamas in the early-morning and late-night scenes. Recurring prop: his chipped white diner mug with one faded red stripe, from her hometown diner.
Other people: the chief of staff (tall Black man in his late forties, shaved head, rimless glasses, white shirt with rolled sleeves, loosened navy tie, printed schedule on a clipboard); the military aide (young white officer in his early thirties, short sandy hair, dark-blue service uniform with no readable insignia, a black leather satchel in one hand); the Secret Service agent (Latina woman in her late thirties, dark hair in a tight bun, dark suit, coiled earpiece cord, small lapel pin); the hero's wife (white-haired woman around sixty, short white curls, soft green cardigan, reading glasses pushed up on her head).

## COLD OPEN
1. Five forty-five in the morning. You wake before the alarm in a house with one hundred thirty-two rooms and pad to the window. Down on the lawn, in the dark, a man in a dark coat stands very still. He has been there all night.
2. He is not guarding a person. He is guarding the President of the United States. That's you. You pour coffee into a chipped mug from your hometown diner. It's the only thing here that still uses your first name.
## REWIND
3. To understand why, rewind to a cold January noon: one hand on a book, one hand raised, an oath of thirty-five words. Then somebody opened a car door for you. You have not opened one yourself since.
## CHAPTER 1 — THE FOLDER
4. Seven a.m. A leather folder waits on your desk: the President's Daily Brief, the intelligence services' summary of what you should know before lunch. Some version of it has been put in front of presidents since 1946.
5. You read it the way other people read the weather. Except this weather covers the whole planet, and none of it is sunny. Sunny news doesn't need a folder. Your coffee goes cold by page three. It always does.
6. Eight a.m. Your chief of staff brings the schedule, cut into fifteen-minute blocks. Thirty-one of them. Your name is on every one. Lunch has been shortened twice. He apologises for lunch. He apologises for lunch every day.
7. And there is one person you are never more than a few steps from: a military aide with a black leather satchel. About forty-five pounds. It goes where you go, elevators included. What's inside is not discussed. Not by you, not by us.
## CHAPTER 2 — THE DESK
8. Nine a.m. Meetings. Here is how the desk works: easy problems get solved downstairs. Only the ones nobody else could solve come up to you. So everything on this desk is hard. That's not a bad day. That's the design.
9. Ten fifteen. A decision. You listen, you ask, you weigh, you choose. Whatever you choose, somebody will be unhappy, and that somebody will have a microphone. You choose anyway. Nobody else is allowed to.
10. Then you sign things. Lots of things. Under the Presidential Records Act of 1978, your official papers are not yours. They belong to the public. Every memo, every note in the margin. So you doodle very carefully.
11. Eleven a.m. The mail. Tens of thousands of letters, faxes and emails reach the White House every day, asking you to fix a pothole, a roof, a cat. A whole office reads them. A small stack reaches you. You read every one.
12. Twelve forty. Lunch: a sandwich at your desk, eleven minutes. If this sounds familiar, you've met the nurse, the courier, the teacher, the cook, the trucker, the flight attendant, the firefighter and the construction worker. Same calendar deciding when you eat. Thirty-five bathrooms. No time for any of them.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE BUBBLE
13. Two p.m. An event across town. You get in the back of the car, because presidents do not drive on public roads. The Secret Service drives. The last time you drove, you had to find parking. You miss parking.
14. The windows don't open. The route was planned days ago by people you've never met. You pass a coffee shop with a line out the door. You would love to stand in that line. You don't do lines anymore.
15. The agent beside you has an earpiece, a dark suit and one job: to stand between you and danger. She is calm, polite, never more than a door away. She knows how you take your coffee. She has never asked how your day is going. That's professionalism.
16. At the event you shake two hundred hands, about four seconds each. Every one of those people will remember their four seconds forever. You will remember almost none, and it bothers you every single time.
17. The quiet part of the job: almost everyone you meet needs something from you. That's not cynical. It's the job description. So when someone asks how you slept, and actually waits for the answer, you notice. It's usually your wife.
## CHAPTER 4 — THE BODY
18. Here is what the chair does to the body. A 2015 study in the BMJ compared elected heads of government with the runners-up they beat, in seventeen countries, over almost three hundred years. The winners lived about two point seven years less.
19. The researchers' careful conclusion: elected leaders may indeed age more quickly. You didn't need a study. You have a mirror. The silver arrived in year one and has been asking for more territory ever since.
20. The pay, by law: four hundred thousand dollars a year, plus a fifty-thousand-dollar expense allowance. It sounds like a lot. Spread it over a job with no evenings, no weekends and no off switch, and it's a very well-paid night shift.
21. And the groceries are yours. By long tradition, the President pays for the family's personal food, billed every month, itemised. The kitchen can serve dinner to one hundred forty guests. Your sandwich is on the bill.
## CHAPTER 5 — THE NIGHT
22. Six thirty. Dinner upstairs with your wife. For forty minutes, nobody says "Mr. President." She uses the name on your library card, steals your fries and complains about the crossword. Best forty minutes of the day.
23. Eight p.m. A thick binder arrives: tomorrow. You read it in an armchair with the chipped mug, now full of tea. Tomorrow has thirty-four blocks. Somebody has already apologised for tomorrow's lunch.
24. Ten p.m. The phone. Something has happened on the other side of the world, where it's mid-afternoon, and the world does not check your time zone. You listen. You ask three questions. You decide. Then you find your place in the binder.
25. Eleven thirty. This house has four hundred twelve doors and one hundred forty-seven windows. You have time for exactly one. The man is still on the lawn. Somewhere in the building, the aide with the satchel is awake. A country sleeps because somebody doesn't.
## CHAPTER 6 — WHY
26. Some days the job feels like a very long meeting in a very beautiful house. Then a letter arrives from a nine-year-old asking if the President has a dog. You answer it yourself, by hand. It goes in the records. Forever.
27. So why do it. Because almost every decision at that desk lands on somebody you will never meet. A bridge gets fixed. A family gets an answer. A storm gets a response. You never see it land. You sign anyway.
28. And because the job isn't yours. It was here before you, and it will be here after. You are borrowing the desk, the house, the folder, even the man on the lawn. Every paper belongs to the public. The mug, legally, is yours.
29. One day the oath will belong to someone else. You'll walk out of a door you didn't open, into a car you're not allowed to drive, and go home. And the first thing you'll do is stand in a line. Any line. On purpose.
30. That was one day. Tomorrow starts at five forty-five, at the window, with the folder. Next time: a different job, a different door. Subscribe if you want to be there. And if you ever write to the President, keep it short. Lunch is eleven minutes.

## Sources
- Salary: $400,000 a year, paid monthly, plus a $50,000 expense allowance for official duties — 3 U.S.C. §102 (Cornell LII text of the U.S. Code).
- President's Daily Brief: "presented in some form to the president since 1946," a "daily summary of strategic, all-source intelligence," produced by ODNI for the president and key cabinet members and advisers — Office of the Director of National Intelligence, Mission Integration page (current). Background: CIA, first issue of the modern brief on 17 June 1961 (CIA PDB release keynote, 2015).
- Military aide: a military aide carrying the emergency satchel ("football") is "never more than a few steps away, even in elevators"; five aides rotate the duty; the satchel weighs about 45 lb when filled — Atomic Heritage Foundation / National Museum of Nuclear Science & History, "Nuclear Briefcases".
- Residence: 132 rooms, 35 bathrooms and 6 levels; 412 doors, 147 windows, 28 fireplaces, 8 staircases, 3 elevators; the kitchen can serve dinner to as many as 140 guests — whitehouse.gov, "The White House" (About the White House), current.
- Mail: "Tens of thousands of letters, faxes, and emails from Americans across the country arrive each day" at the Office of Presidential Correspondence — Director of Presidential Correspondence, official White House blog, 3 Aug 2009 (archived at archives.gov). No current official daily count found, so the script keeps the phrasing "tens of thousands".
- Health: elected heads of government lived 2.7 fewer years than runners-up after adjustment (95% CI 0.6 to 4.8; 4.4 years unadjusted), hazard ratio 1.23; 540 candidates (279 winners, 261 runners-up) in 17 countries, 1722–2015; authors: "elected leaders may indeed age more quickly" — Olenski, Abola & Jena, "Do heads of government age more quickly?", BMJ 2015;351:h6424 (Christmas issue, observational).
- Records: the Presidential Records Act of 1978 (44 U.S.C. 2201–2209) "changed the legal ownership of the official records of the President from private to public"; records pass automatically to the Archivist when the President leaves office; applies to records created after 20 Jan 1981 — National Archives, "Presidential Records Act (PRA) of 1978".
- Common practice, not statistics: presidents do not drive on public roads (long-standing Secret Service practice, not a statute); the President and family pay for personal food, billed monthly and itemised by the usher's office (long-standing practice, White House Historical Association chief historian via AP, 2014); the 35-word oath is the text of Article II, Section 1 (counting without "or affirm"); inauguration at noon in January (20th Amendment). The 5:45 wake-up, fifteen-minute schedule blocks, the eleven-minute lunch, two hundred handshakes, the agent on the lawn and the evening briefing binder are the hero's fictional day, not measured figures.
