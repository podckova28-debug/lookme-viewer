# Why It's So Hard to Be an Underwater Welder — Episode 13 (~7 min, 27 blocks)

## COLD OPEN
1. Sixty feet under the Gulf of Mexico, it is so dark you cannot see your own glove in front of your mask. It is cold, it is loud, and you are about to start a fire. Underwater.
2. Underwater welders fix what the sea breaks: oil platforms, pipelines, bridges, dams and the hulls of ships. People online call it the most dangerous job in the world. That is not quite true. But it is close enough to matter.
## REWIND — DIVE SCHOOL
3. To see why, rewind to dive school. Programs run from about two to seven months, and tuition alone is often fifteen to twenty-four thousand dollars. Room, food and gear come on top.
4. You learn helmets and hoses, decompression tables, rigging, cutting and welding. Then you learn to do all of it again with your eyes closed, in a tank of murky water, because that is what the real job looks like.
## CHAPTER 1 — THE TENDER
5. You graduate, and you are not a diver yet. You are a tender. That is the bottom rung of the ladder, and everybody starts there.
6. A tender carries everything, coils hoses, dresses the diver and watches the bubbles. The diver's hose is called the umbilical. It carries air, a radio line, hot water and sometimes power. If it tangles, someone can die. So you watch it like a hawk.
7. The work is offshore, on a barge, for weeks at a time. Twelve-hour shifts. A bunk in a room with three strangers. The sea moves all night, and some mornings your stomach moves with it.
8. Then one morning, after months of tending, the supervisor looks at you and says the words you have been waiting for. You're diving.
## CHAPTER 2 — GOING DOWN
9. Your helmet weighs about thirty pounds on deck. Underwater it floats. Your suit is pumped full of hot water from the surface, because at depth the cold can take a strong person apart in minutes.
10. You go down a line. At thirty feet the light turns green. At sixty it is almost gone. At thirty-three feet the pressure on your body has already doubled. Every breath you take is packed tighter than the one before.
11. Down here, visibility is often zero. You work by feel. You find the crack with your fingertips. You learn to read a steel beam the way other people read a book.
12. Up top, the supervisor watches you through a camera on your helmet and talks to you the whole time. You talk back. When a diver goes quiet, the whole deck goes quiet too.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE WELD
13. Now the weld. You hold a rod in a stinger, a welding handle wrapped in insulation. When the arc strikes, it boils the water around it into a pocket of gas and steam. For a moment it is the only light in the ocean.
14. You never switch the power on yourself. You say, make it hot, and someone on deck closes the switch. When you're done, you say, make it cold. Hundreds of amps run through that rod. You do not let it touch you while it is live.
15. The cold water also cools the weld too fast, which can make it brittle. So the most important welds are often done dry, inside a pressurized box built around the pipe, called a habitat. You weld in air, sixty feet down.
## CHAPTER 4 — THE DANGER
16. Here is the number you will see online: fifteen percent of underwater welders die. That is a myth. But the real number is not small.
17. Federal safety researchers counted about one hundred fifty-nine deaths per hundred thousand commercial divers per year. That is roughly one in six hundred, every year. A study in Alaska found divers were dying at forty times the average rate for all workers.
18. What kills divers is drowning, tangled hoses, failed gear, and something called Delta P. When water rushes toward an opening, like a pipe intake or a dam gate, it can pin a diver with enormous force. You cannot see it coming. That is why the crew checks for it before every dive.
19. Then there are the bends. At depth, your body soaks up gas from what you breathe. Come up too fast and it fizzes out as bubbles in your joints, your blood, even your brain.
20. So you come up slowly, stopping on the line, hanging in the dark and waiting. Sometimes you finish inside a steel chamber on the deck, while the crew eats dinner on the other side of a small round window.
## CHAPTER 5 — SATURATION
21. The top rung is saturation diving. When the work is too deep to come up every day, you live under pressure in a steel chamber on the ship for up to twenty-eight days.
22. You breathe a mix of helium and oxygen. It makes everyone sound like a cartoon. Food comes in through a little airlock. A diving bell carries you down to the job, and back to the chamber to sleep.
23. Going home takes days. From six hundred fifty feet, decompression can take about eight days, sealed inside the chamber. You cannot leave early. Your body has to let the gas out at its own pace.
## CHAPTER 6 — THE MONEY AND THE LIFE
24. And the money? The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics put the median pay for commercial divers at about sixty-one thousand dollars a year. Saturation divers can earn much more, but only for the days they spend under pressure.
25. You miss birthdays. You call home from a barge with a bad connection. You come back sunburned, tired and a little deaf, and your kid asks why you smell like diesel.
## WHY
26. So why does anyone do it? Because bridges have to stand, pipelines cannot leak, and ships cannot sink. Somebody has to go down where nobody can see, and fix it by touch.
27. Nobody will ever see your best weld. It is sixty feet down, in the dark, holding something together. Would you do this job for sixty thousand a year? Tell us in the comments. And if you liked this, subscribe.
