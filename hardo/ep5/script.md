# Your Life as a Plague Doctor — Episode 5 (~6–7 min, 30 blocks)

Style: Dinzo. White background, flat vector, second person, deadpan. Narrator: Chatterbox + Kokoro reference (bm_george), tempo x1.2.
Setting: Rome, plague of 1656–57 (the beak suit is a 17th-century Italian/French thing, not the Black Death of 1348).
TTS rules: numbers and years as words, no "often".

## COLD OPEN
1. Rome, the summer of sixteen fifty-six. Two in the morning. You are standing outside a house with its door nailed shut, wearing a leather bird mask stuffed with dried roses. Inside, a family of six is waiting for you to save them. The medicine that could actually do it won't exist for nearly three hundred years.
2. Your beak smells like a funeral in a flower shop. Your coat is waxed leather, and it is ninety degrees. You knock with a long wooden cane, because this job has one rule: don't touch anyone. Tonight you'll find out how long you can keep it.
## REWIND
3. So how did you end up in a bird costume at two a.m.? Rewind three months, to the day the city came to you with a contract, and you said yes. You will think about that yes a lot.
## CHAPTER 1 — THE CONTRACT
4. Spring. The plague is in Naples, a few days' ride south. Rome shuts its gates, posts guards on the roads, and starts hiring. Not the great doctors. The great doctors have country houses. Rome needs someone who will stay.
5. That's you. Young, not famous, short on patients. The city offers a monthly salary, paid by the city, which tells you something. In return, you treat everyone, rich or poor, and you do not leave until it's over.
6. This is not a safe job. In Edinburgh, in sixteen forty-five, the city hired a plague doctor named John Paulitious. Within months, he was dead. His replacement, George Rae, asked for a much bigger salary. He got it. Remember him. He comes back later.
## CHAPTER 2 — THE SUIT
7. Now, the outfit. A waxed leather coat down to your ankles. Leather gloves, leather boots, leather hat. Round glass eyes. And the beak: half a foot long, packed with herbs.
8. Here's the logic. Everyone knows disease travels through bad air. Miasma: a stink rising from rot, swamps and corpses. So you breathe through a filter of roses, mint, cloves, camphor and myrrh. If you can't smell death, death can't get in.
9. The logic is wrong. Plague is a bacterium, carried by fleas that ride on rats and on people. Nobody will prove it until eighteen ninety-four. But a slick waxed coat may make it harder for a flea to bite. Historians aren't sure. You certainly aren't.
10. Quick fact check. The beak suit belongs to the sixteen hundreds, in Italy and France. The doctors of the Black Death, three hundred years before you, had no beak. They had a robe and a prayer. You are the upgrade.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE ROUNDS
11. Six a.m. You get your list. A house with a sick person in it is sealed: door nailed shut, a guard outside, food passed in through a window. Your job is to go in.
12. You check the signs. Fever. Vomiting. And the buboes: swollen lymph nodes in the groin, the armpit or the neck, as big as an egg. If you see them, the house goes on the list, and the list goes to the health office by noon.
13. The health office decides the rest. The sick go to the plague hospital on the Tiber Island. Their families go into quarantine. Their mattresses get burned. Nobody is happy to see you. Kids run from the bird. You'd run from the bird too.
14. Noon. It's ninety degrees inside the coat. You can't wipe your face, because your face is inside a bird. Sweat runs down behind the glass eyes. You see the whole city through a fog of rose water and fear.
15. You also write wills. People dying of plague still need to leave the vineyard to someone. You stand in the doorway, take notes through a beak, and witness last wishes for strangers. Nobody trained you for this part either.
## CHAPTER 4 — THE MEDICINE
16. Now, the medicine. You have three tools. Bloodletting, to balance the humors. Theriac, a paste of more than sixty ingredients, including, yes, viper flesh. And a small knife, to open the buboes and drain them.
17. Opening a bubo is the most dangerous thing you do all day. It's also the one treatment that sometimes seems to help. You lean in, close, and for about four seconds, the cane rule is gone.
18. Doctors in other places tried other things. Sponges soaked in vinegar. Smoke. Pressing a live chicken to the swelling, plucked bottom first. No, really. None of it worked. Everyone was trying anyway.
19. Here is the real number. Without antibiotics, bubonic plague kills somewhere between thirty and sixty percent of the people who catch it. So on a good week, about half your patients live, and you have no idea why.
## CHAPTER 5 — THE PRICE
20. Here's the part nobody paints on the poster. You can't go home. Your family could catch it from you. So you live alone, you eat alone, and people cross the street when they see the coat coming.
21. Every night you take off the suit and check yourself. Armpits. Neck. Groin. Every night you're fine. You know that one night you might not be. You remember John Paulitious.
22. And the money. Back in Edinburgh, George Rae was promised a hundred pounds Scots a month. He survived. Then the plague ended, the city decided that was a lot of money, and he spent years asking to be paid. The bird always gets stiffed.
## CHAPTER 6 — THE END
23. Summer, sixteen fifty-seven. The plague leaves Rome. The city has lost somewhere around fifteen thousand people. Naples, which reacted late, lost around half its population. One big reason Rome did better: it sealed, counted and quarantined, fast.
24. That's you. The nailed doors, the lists, the island. The bird mask didn't do much. The boring paperwork did.
25. You take off the beak for the last time. The roses inside went brown back in June. There is a dent in your face from the strap. You look in a mirror, and for the first time in months, a person looks back.
## CHAPTER 7 — WHY
26. So why do it. Not the money; you might never see it. Not the cure; you never had one. You did it because someone had to walk into those houses, and the city asked, and you said yes.
27. And because of the ones who lived. A baker on the Via Giulia. A girl, eight years old, who called you "the bird," and later, "doctor." You'll never know if you saved them. They'll never forget that you knocked.
28. Three hundred years later, your mask will be a Halloween costume, a carnival mask in Venice, a museum poster. Nobody will remember the paperwork. Everybody will remember the bird.
29. Today, a few days of antibiotics cure plague. It still exists: a few hundred cases a year, mostly in Madagascar, the Congo and Peru. The doctors who fight it now wear gloves and masks. No beak.
30. That was one life. Next time: a different century, a different door. Subscribe if you want to step through it. And if a man in a bird mask knocks at two in the morning, let him in. He's on your side.
