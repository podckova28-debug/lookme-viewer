# Why It's So Hard to Be a Truck Driver — Episode 5 (~6 min, 30 blocks)

Style: Dinzo. White background, flat vector, second person, deadpan. Narrator: cheapest engine, tempo x1.15.
Hero: long-haul driver, woman in her forties, orange high-vis vest over a grey hoodie, dark baseball cap, brown braid.

## COLD OPEN
1. Four fifty in the morning. Your alarm is your phone, your bed is a shelf behind two seats, and your window is a rest area in Nebraska. You have eleven hours of driving today. Your clock started the second you touched the steering wheel.
2. You are not late. You are never late. You are legally forbidden from being late, because the little black box on your dashboard counts every minute you work, and when it says stop, you stop. Wherever you are.
## REWIND
3. To understand why you sleep in a parking lot by choice, rewind to yesterday morning, when you still had a plan, a full thermos, and no idea where you'd be tonight.
## CHAPTER 1 — THE INSPECTION
4. Five a.m. Before you drive one foot, you walk around eighty thousand pounds of truck with a flashlight. Tires, brakes, lights, mirrors, the fifth wheel. Eighteen tires. You kick none of them. You check all of them.
5. The trailer is fifty-three feet long. Your truck is seventy-two feet from bumper to bumper. You will park this thing tonight in a space designed by someone who has never parked anything.
6. You log on. The black box is called an electronic logging device, and it knows four things about you: driving, on duty, off duty, sleeper. It does not know "stuck." It does not know "hungry." It only knows time.
7. The rules fit on a card. Eleven hours of driving. A fourteen-hour window to do it in. A thirty-minute break by hour eight. Seventy hours in eight days, then thirty-four hours off. You know this card better than your own phone number.
## CHAPTER 2 — THE DOCK
8. Seven thirty. The warehouse. Your appointment is at seven thirty. You check in at seven thirty. A man in a vest says, "Dock twelve, we'll call you." Nobody calls you.
9. You wait. Your fourteen-hour window does not wait. It started at five. Every minute at this dock is a minute you will not have on the highway tonight, and the black box knows it.
10. Nine forty. Two hours and ten minutes. Officially, this is now "detention." Drivers report it at about four in ten stops. You are paid by the mile. The last two hours paid exactly zero miles.
11. Your company will send the shipper a bill for your time. The shipper will pay about half of those bills. You will see none of it either way. You are, technically, furniture in this parking lot.
12. Ten fifteen. "Dock twelve." You back fifty-three feet of trailer into a slot with two inches on each side, using two mirrors and a feeling. You do it in one try. Nobody claps. You've stopped expecting it.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE ROAD
13. Ten forty. Finally, the highway. Interstate eighty, westbound. Your speed is sixty-five. Your mind is on tonight. You have about six hours left in the window and five hundred miles of wanting.
14. You do not go to the bathroom. If this sounds familiar, you've met the nurse, the courier, the teacher and the cook. Same bladder, bigger vehicle.
15. Lunch is a gas station sandwich eaten at the wheel, because stopping costs you thirty minutes and thirty minutes is forty miles. There is a cooler behind your seat with a plan in it. The plan was salad.
16. Here is what the body pays. In 2023, eight hundred twenty-three truck drivers died on the job in America. More than any other occupation. Not the most dangerous rate, the most people. You are one of about two million of them.
17. Your thirty-minute break. The law says by hour eight. You take it at a rest area, in the sleeper, eyes closed, not asleep. The clock is paused. It is the only time all day the clock is paused.
18. The GPS is lying again. It wants you on a bridge with a twelve-foot clearance. Your trailer is thirteen and a half. You know this because you check, every time, because someone, somewhere, once didn't.
## CHAPTER 4 — THE MONEY
19. The median truck driver in America makes about fifty-seven thousand four hundred forty dollars a year. That's twenty-seven dollars and sixty-two cents an hour, if you only count the hours they count.
20. But you are not paid by the hour. You are paid by the mile. So the dock paid nothing, the inspection paid nothing, the break paid nothing, and the hour you'll spend looking for parking tonight will pay nothing. Your truck earns. You wait.
21. At the big carriers, turnover has run around ninety percent a year for most of a decade. Nine out of ten seats, new person. The trucks don't change. The drivers do.
## CHAPTER 5 — PARKING
22. Five fifteen p.m. The hunt. You have about an hour of legal driving left, and you need a parking space for a seventy-two-foot vehicle in a country that forgot to build them.
23. Drivers spend on average fifty-six minutes a day looking for parking. Almost an hour of your eleven. That's about forty-six hundred dollars a year of driving time, spent circling lots that are already full.
24. The first truck stop: full. The second: full, and someone has parked a pickup across two spaces. The third has one spot, at the end, next to a dumpster, nose to the highway. It's yours. It's beautiful.
25. Six twenty. You stop. Not because you want to. Because the box says so. Your fourteen-hour window closes at seven, and you will be inside it, parked, with eleven minutes to spare.
## CHAPTER 6 — THE NIGHT
26. The shower costs a token you get for buying fuel. You buy eighty gallons of fuel. The shower is the best eight minutes of your day. You rate it. Four stars. The water pressure.
27. Dinner is a microwave burrito eaten on the shelf you sleep on, watching a phone propped against a pillow. Somebody on it is explaining why truckers are lonely. You hit the like button. You don't finish it.
28. Ten p.m. You call home. Someone says the dog did a thing. You laugh at the right place. You are two thousand miles from the dog. You will be home Saturday. Probably. The box decides.
## CHAPTER 7 — WHY
29. So why do it. Because somewhere behind you is a warehouse that was empty and now isn't. Because the highway at five a.m. with the sun coming up over Wyoming is the best view in any office in America. Because you are very, very good at backing up.
30. That was one day. Tomorrow starts at four fifty, with a flashlight and eighteen tires. Next time: a different job, a different door. Subscribe if you want to be there. And if you see a truck looking for parking tonight, that pickup across two spaces? Move it.
