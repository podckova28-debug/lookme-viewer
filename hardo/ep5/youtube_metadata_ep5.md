## Description
Rome, 1656. Two in the morning. You're standing outside a house with its door nailed shut, in a leather bird mask stuffed with dried roses, and the family inside is waiting for you to save them.

This is one plague season as a plague doctor, lived from the inside: the city contract nobody else wanted, the waxed coat and the herb-filled beak (and why the theory behind it was wrong), the sealed houses, the lists for the health office, the quarantine island on the Tiber, theriac made with viper flesh, the one rule of the job — don't touch anyone — and the four seconds a day you break it.

Based on real history:
• The beaked suit is a 17th-century Italian and French costume, not something from the Black Death of 1348 — Paul Fürst's 1656 engraving "Doctor Schnabel von Rom" (British Museum)
• Doctors believed disease spread through bad air (miasma), so the beak was filled with aromatics; the real cause, Yersinia pestis, was identified by Alexandre Yersin in 1894
• Edinburgh, 1645: plague doctor John Paulitious died in post; his successor George Rae was promised £100 Scots a month and struggled for years to get paid
• Rome in 1656–57 sealed infected houses, posted guards and used Tiber Island as a plague hospital; Naples lost about half its population
• Untreated bubonic plague kills 30–60% of patients; today it is cured with common antibiotics — World Health Organization
