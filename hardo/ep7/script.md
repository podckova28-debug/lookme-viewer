# Why It's So Hard to Be a Firefighter — Episode 7 (~6 min, 30 blocks)

Style: the cook look (ep4): thin line, realistic proportions, soft flat colour, hero + one prop on pure white. Narrator: Grady (ElevenLabs), tempo x1.1.
Hero: career firefighter, woman in her mid-thirties, Black, short natural hair, navy station t-shirt with a small blank patch, navy work trousers with a radio on the belt, black boots; turnout gear (tan coat and pants with yellow reflective stripes, black helmet) in the fire scenes.

## COLD OPEN
1. Two eleven in the morning. The tones drop. You are asleep, then you are standing, then you are in your boots, and your brain has not been consulted about any of it. The light in the bay is already on. It is always already on.
2. Forty-five seconds from bed to the truck. You have done this about four thousand times. Your body knows where every strap is in the dark. Your body would like to speak to a manager.
## REWIND
3. To understand why you sleep in your clothes next to a pole, rewind to seven a.m., when you walked into the station with a coffee and a plan, and the plan lasted eleven minutes.
## CHAPTER 1 — THE SHIFT
4. Seven a.m. Shift change. You work twenty-four hours straight, then forty-eight off. Some stations do forty-eight on, ninety-six off. Either way it comes to about fifty-six hours a week, and the week does not care which day it is.
5. First hour: the rig check. Every hose, every nozzle, every air bottle, every saw. You start the saws. All of them. The neighbours have opinions about this at seven fifteen. You have never once met the neighbours.
6. Your gear: a tan coat, pants, a hood, gloves, a helmet, and an air pack. All of it together, roughly seventy pounds. You will put it on in under a minute, in the dark, in a truck that is moving.
## CHAPTER 2 — THE CALLS
7. Here is what nobody outside the station knows: most of your calls are not fires. Not even close. Of the thirty-six million calls US fire departments ran in a year, about one in twenty-seven was a fire. Almost three in four were medical.
8. So the first call is a man on a kitchen floor with chest pain. You check his pulse, start oxygen, and hold his wife's hand while she looks for his pills. No smoke. No ladder. This is the job. The posters lie a little.
9. Second call: a car into a pole. Nobody hurt, one airbag, a lot of glass. You sweep the glass. Somebody has to sweep the glass. You've swept more glass than any janitor in the county.
10. Third call: a smoke alarm with a dead battery at a house with a very loud dog. You change the battery. You pet the dog. The dog does not know you saved its life. The dog is fine with this.
11. You do not go to the bathroom. If this sounds familiar, you've met the nurse, the courier, the teacher, the cook, the trucker and the flight attendant. Same bladder, louder alarm.
12. Lunch is at the station, at eleven, because you've learned that one o'clock lunch gets eaten at four. You cook for seven people. The chili is good. The tones drop at the second spoon. The chili is cold at four.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE FIRE
13. Three forty p.m. A real one. Smoke showing from a second-floor window, a woman on the lawn saying her son is upstairs. Your heart does something. You let it. Then you go in.
14. Inside, there is no fire like in the movies. There is black. Total black, hot enough to feel through the gloves, and a sound like a highway. You find the stairs with your feet. You find the boy with your hands.
15. Here is what the body pays. In 2022 the World Health Organization's cancer agency put firefighting in Group 1: carcinogenic to humans. Same group as asbestos. The smoke you just breathed will be with you for years.
16. A long NIOSH study of thirty thousand firefighters found about nine percent more cancer diagnoses and fourteen percent more cancer deaths than the general public. You know these numbers. You wash your hood anyway. It helps. A little.
17. The boy is fine. He was in the closet with a cat. You hand him to his mother, and the cat to nobody, because the cat has left. You will not be thanked for the cat. You understand.
18. Four fifty. You sit on the bumper. Your shirt under the coat is wet through. You drink a bottle of water in one go and the captain says "good job," and that is the whole ceremony. That is all the ceremony you need.
## CHAPTER 4 — THE MONEY
19. The median firefighter in America makes about fifty-nine thousand five hundred dollars a year. For fifty-six hours a week, that's about twenty dollars an hour. The guy who fixed your furnace charged more. He also got to sleep.
20. And here's the part the city forgets: most firefighters in America are not paid at all. About two in three are volunteers. They leave dinner, they leave work, they leave a kid's game, and they go in. Then they go back to dinner.
## CHAPTER 5 — THE NIGHT
21. Nine p.m. Dishes. Then the gear: wash the hood, scrub the coat, swap the air bottle, check the saw you already checked. The station is quiet. Quiet makes you nervous. Quiet never lasts.
22. Ten thirty. You lie down in your clothes. Boots next to the bed, pants folded over them, so you step in and pull up in one move. You do not fall asleep. You lower yourself into a shallow version of it, with one ear open.
23. Eleven fifty. Tones. A woman who fell in the bathroom. You lift her, check her, make tea, because that is what she needs, and you are back by twelve forty. You lie down again. One ear.
24. Two eleven. Tones. You already know this one. It's the cold open. Forty-five seconds. Boots, pants, pole, truck. Your body does it. You just come along.
25. It's a dumpster. A dumpster on fire behind a grocery store, at two in the morning, with nobody around and a raccoon watching from a distance. You put it out in six minutes. The raccoon is unimpressed. So are you.
26. Four a.m. Back in the bunk. Two hours of sleep in twenty-one hours. Studies say that's about the same as being legally drunk. You will drive a forty-thousand-pound truck at seven. Nobody has a better idea.
## CHAPTER 6 — WHY
27. Seven a.m. Shift change. You hand over the radio, the rig, and the chili. You drive home with the windows down to get the smoke out of your hair. It doesn't come out. It hasn't come out in nine years.
28. So why do it. Because a boy is in a closet and you are the one who finds him. Because an old woman gets tea. Because when the tones drop, your body goes, and some small part of you is proud of a body that goes.
29. And because the station table at eleven, with seven people and a pot of chili and the saws finally quiet, is the best office in the world. For about eleven minutes.
30. That was one day. Well, one day and one night. Next time: a different job, a different door. Subscribe if you want to be there. And please: change the battery. The dog can't.

## Sources
- Median pay $59,530 a year, $28.62 an hour; about 345,000 jobs — BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, Firefighters, May 2024.
- Shifts: 24/48 and 48/96 schedules, about 56 hours a week — IAFF shift-work documents, FirefighterNow (secondary).
- Calls: 36.6 million fire department calls in 2021; 1.35 million fires (about 1 in 27); 26.3 million medical (72%) — NFPA Fire Department Calls, 2021 data.
- Cancer: IARC Monographs vol. 132 (2022) classified occupational exposure as a firefighter as Group 1; NIOSH cohort of about 30,000 career firefighters (Daniels et al., 2013/2014) found about 9% more cancer diagnoses and 14% more cancer deaths than the general US population.
- Volunteers: about 65% of US firefighters are volunteers; 635,000 volunteers in 2023 — National Volunteer Fire Council fact sheet (NFPA data).
- Gear weight (~70 lb with SCBA) and 45-second turnout time are industry rules of thumb, not statistics; the 2-hours-of-sleep / impairment comparison refers to the Dawson & Reid (1997) fatigue studies and is stated as "studies say".
