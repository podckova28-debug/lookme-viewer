# Why It's So Hard to Be a Flight Attendant — Episode 6 (~6 min, 30 blocks)

Style: original series look (episodes 3–4): white background, thin clean lines, soft flat colour, second person, deadpan. Narrator: Piper lessac (free), tempo x1.1.
Hero: flight attendant, man in his late twenties, short black hair, white shirt, navy waistcoat, burgundy tie, blank name tag, black rolling suitcase.

## COLD OPEN
1. Three fifty in the morning. The alarm is in a hotel room, the hotel is in a city you had to check on your phone, and your uniform is hanging on the bathroom door because the iron doesn't work. You have four flights today. You've been paid for none of them yet.
2. Your name tag says a name. Your face says three fifty. In two hours, two hundred strangers will ask you where to put a bag that is clearly too big, and you will smile at every one of them. The smile is not in your contract. It is, however, expected.
## REWIND
3. To understand how you ended up in a hotel in a city you can't name, rewind to yesterday afternoon, when your phone rang in the grocery store and a voice said "reserve." You put the groceries back.
## CHAPTER 1 — REPORT
4. Four forty-five. Report time. You badge through a door passengers never see, and you're on duty. Officially. The clock that matters to the airline has started. The clock that pays you has not.
5. The briefing takes six minutes: four legs, a tight turn in Denver, one wheelchair in row twelve, one nut allergy in row twenty, and a captain who says "we'll try to make up time." You've heard that sentence maybe four thousand times.
6. You check the emergency equipment. Oxygen, extinguishers, the defibrillator, the slides. Every row. Every flight. You've never used most of it. You check it like you will. That is the whole point.
## CHAPTER 2 — BOARDING
7. Five forty. Boarding. Here is the thing nobody outside the airplane knows: for most of the history of this job, this part was free. Your hourly pay started when the door closed. Boarding, the hardest forty minutes of the flight, was a gift to the airline.
8. That is changing. Delta started paying half rate for boarding in 2022. American started in April 2025. United's new contract includes it too. So this morning you are either paid for the next forty minutes or you are not, depending on your logo. Same bags. Same bins.
9. The bins. A man with a bag the size of a dishwasher is explaining that it fit last time. You know it did not. You help him anyway, because the alternative is a conversation, and a conversation costs two minutes you don't have.
10. Row twelve needs the wheelchair. Row twenty needs you to announce the nut allergy. Row thirty-one needs to know if the flight has WiFi. It does. It will not work. You say "it should."
11. Six ten. The door closes. Somewhere, in a computer in another state, your pay begins. You take a breath. It is the only breath you'll take sitting down until the seat belt sign goes off.
12. The safety demonstration. You point at the exits. Nobody looks. You hold up the seat belt. Nobody looks. You do it like it matters, because once, on a different plane, it did. You remember that flight. The passengers don't.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE AIR
13. The drink cart weighs about two hundred pounds loaded. You push it uphill, because the plane is climbing, down an aisle seventeen inches wide, past elbows, knees, and one bare foot. The foot is always there. The foot is universal.
14. You do not go to the bathroom. If this sounds familiar, you've met the nurse, the courier, the teacher, the cook and the trucker. Same bladder, thirty-five thousand feet.
15. The seat belt sign comes on. The captain says "light chop." You are standing with a pot of coffee. Here is what the body pays: between 2009 and 2021 the FAA counted one hundred forty-six serious turbulence injuries on US airlines. One hundred sixteen of them, almost eighty percent, were crew. Not passengers. You. Standing. With the coffee.
16. You sit on the jump seat, strap in, and watch the cabin bounce. A passenger unbuckles to get his headphones. You say "sir." He sits. You've learned to say "sir" in a tone that stops grown men. It took years.
17. Landing. Denver. Twenty-eight minutes until the next flight leaves. In that time two hundred people get off, the cabin gets cleaned, and two hundred new people get on. The cleaning is partly you. Nobody mentioned that in the interview either.
18. You find three newspapers, one diaper, forty-one plastic cups, a phone, a passport, and half a sandwich in a seat pocket. The phone and the passport go back to their people, one of them running. You've stopped asking why. Why doesn't help you clean.
## CHAPTER 4 — THE RULES
19. The law lets the airline schedule you for fourteen hours on duty. Then it owes you ten hours of rest. Ten, not nine, since 2022, and the airline can no longer cut it to eight. Those ten hours include the van, the hotel check-in, the shower, and finding food after eleven p.m. Sleep is what's left.
20. One flight attendant for every fifty seats. That's the rule. On this plane that's four of you for one hundred seventy-six people. If something goes wrong, it's four of you, ninety seconds, and every door. You've practiced it. You hope you never find out.
## CHAPTER 5 — THE MONEY
21. The median flight attendant in America makes about sixty-seven thousand dollars a year. Not bad. But the hours that count are flight hours, roughly seventy-five to ninety a month. The hours in airports, vans and hotels, in uniform, are what the job is actually made of. You are at work far more than you are paid.
22. There's also per diem: a couple of dollars an hour for being away from home. It buys the sandwich at the airport. It does not buy the second one.
23. Leg three. A delay. Weather in Chicago, which is not where you are, but it's where the plane is. You sit at the gate, in uniform, in public, for two hours. The clock that pays you is off. The one that counts your fourteen hours is on.
## CHAPTER 6 — THE LAST LEG
24. Leg four. Night. The cabin is dark, the passengers are asleep, and you stand in the back galley eating crackers off a napkin, looking out the small window at a city that is somebody's home. You know the airport code. Not the city.
25. A kid in row nine is awake and scared. You crouch in the aisle, show her how the window shade works, and give her the pilot wings sticker you keep in your pocket for exactly this. It takes four minutes. It is the best four minutes of your day.
26. Ten forty p.m. Last door opens. "Buh-bye. Buh-bye. Thank you. Buh-bye." You say it one hundred seventy-six times. You mean it about forty.
27. The hotel van. Eleven thirty. Check-in. The iron in this room doesn't work either. You hang the uniform on the bathroom door, set the alarm for three fifty, and eat the second sandwich you didn't buy. Lights out.
## CHAPTER 7 — WHY
28. So why do it. Because when the sign goes off and the sun comes up over a wing at thirty-five thousand feet, you have the best window in the world and nobody in it but you. Because you said "sir" and a man sat down. Because a kid in row nine has your sticker.
29. And because somewhere, right now, about a hundred and thirty thousand of you are doing exactly this, in a hundred cities, smiling at bags that don't fit, and the whole thing works. Nobody films that either.
30. That was one day. Tomorrow starts at three fifty, with an iron that doesn't work. Next time: a different job, a different door. Subscribe if you want to be there. And please: the bag does not fit. It never fit.
