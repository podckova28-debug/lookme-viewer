## Title
Why It's So Hard to Be a Flight Attendant

## Description
It's 3:50 a.m. in a hotel in a city you had to look up. Four flights today. You haven't been paid for any of them yet.

One day as a flight attendant, lived from the inside: report at 4:45, a briefing in six minutes, the emergency check nobody sees, boarding (the part that was free for most of the job's history), a bag the size of a dishwasher, a two-hundred-pound cart pushed uphill, a seat belt sign and a coffee pot, twenty-eight minutes to turn a cabin, a fourteen-hour duty day with ten hours of rest, and a kid in row nine who gets your wings.

Based on real research:
• Median pay for flight attendants: $67,130 a year; about 130,800 jobs — U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook, May 2024
• Pay has historically started when the aircraft door closes; Delta added half-rate boarding pay in 2022, American's contract boarding pay began 1 April 2025, United's new contract includes it — NPR, APFA, CBS Chicago
• FAA rule (October 2022): at least 10 consecutive hours of rest for duty periods of 14 hours or less, with no reductions — Federal Register 87 FR 61988
• 146 serious turbulence injuries on US airlines 2009–2021, 116 of them crew — FAA data via AP, December 2022
• One flight attendant per 50 passenger seats — 14 CFR 121.391
