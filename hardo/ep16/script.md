# The Loneliest Jobs on Earth — Episode 16 (~6 min, 27 blocks)

## COLD OPEN
1. Imagine going to work and not seeing another human face for weeks. No coworkers. No small talk. Just you, the wind, and the job.
2. For some people, that's not a nightmare. It's the job description. Here are ten of the loneliest jobs on Earth, counted down. And at number one, a place so isolated that space agencies use it to practice for Mars.
## #10 — LIGHTHOUSE KEEPER
3. Number ten. The lighthouse keeper. For centuries, keepers lived on rocks in the sea, keeping the lamp burning every night so ships wouldn't crash. On some rock stations, the only way off was a boat, and only when the weather allowed.
4. Then machines took over. Britain's last keepers left North Foreland lighthouse in nineteen ninety-eight. And America's last official keeper, at Boston Light, retired at the end of twenty twenty-three.
## #9 — PENGUIN POSTMASTER
5. Number nine. The penguin postmaster. On a tiny island in Antarctica, there's a post office called Port Lockroy. Every season, a handful of people run it for about five months.
6. They sell stamps to cruise ship passengers, send tens of thousands of postcards, and count the gentoo penguins nesting right outside. There's no running water. And when the ship leaves, it's just you, and a lot of penguins.
## #8 — FIRE LOOKOUT
7. Number eight. The fire lookout. You live in a tiny glass cabin on top of a mountain, all summer, watching the forest for smoke. In nineteen fifty-six, the writer Jack Kerouac did this job for sixty-three days on Desolation Peak, in Washington State.
8. The U.S. Forest Service once had thousands of staffed lookouts. Today, planes, cameras and satellites do most of the watching. But a few hundred towers are still staffed every fire season.
## #7 — SHEEPHERDER
9. Number seven. The sheepherder. In the American West, herders follow huge flocks across mountains and desert for months. Home is a small sheep wagon. The company is a horse and a couple of dogs.
10. Today, most of these herders come from Peru, on temporary work visas. Many say the hardest part isn't the cold or the coyotes. It's having nobody to talk to. Old Basque herders even had a name for going a bit strange out there: struck by sagebrush.
## #6 — LONG-HAUL TRUCKER
11. Number six. The long-haul truck driver. This one might surprise you, because it's a huge job. America has about two point two million heavy truck drivers.
12. But many long-haul drivers spend long stretches on the road, far from home. They eat alone, sleep in the cab, and work nights, weekends and holidays. Surrounded by millions of people, and they know none of them.
## #5 — ISLAND WEATHER OBSERVER
13. Number five. The island weather observer. Gough Island sits in the middle of the South Atlantic, more than two thousand kilometres from the nearest continent. South Africa runs a weather station there.
14. A small team lives on the island for about thirteen months. One ship comes once a year, with new people and fresh food. If you forgot something, you wait until next year.
## #4 — SATURATION DIVER
15. Number four. The saturation diver. These divers repair pipelines deep under the sea. Instead of going up and down every day, they live under pressure in a sealed steel chamber, for up to twenty-eight days.
16. They breathe a mix of helium and oxygen, which makes their voices sound squeaky. And when the job ends, they can't just step outside. Coming back to normal pressure can take several days, sometimes more than a week.
## #3 — SUBMARINER
17. Number three. The submariner. On an American ballistic missile submarine, a patrol usually lasts two to three months, almost all of it underwater. No sunlight. No internet.
18. The whole point is to stay hidden. Families can send short messages, about forty words each. For months, that might be all the news you get from home.
## #2 — SOUTH POLE WINTER CREW
19. Number two. The South Pole winter crew. Every February, the last plane leaves the Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station. Around forty people stay behind.
20. No flights in or out until about October. Six months of darkness. Temperatures that can drop below minus one hundred degrees Fahrenheit. If something goes wrong, help is very, very far away.
## #1 — CONCORDIA WINTER CREW
21. Number one. The winter crew at Concordia. It's a French and Italian station, high on the Antarctic ice. In winter, about a dozen people live there. No plane can reach them, and the nearest humans are six hundred kilometres away.
22. The sun disappears for about four months, and it can get down to minus eighty degrees Celsius. The European Space Agency sends a doctor every year to study the crew. They call it White Mars, because it's practice for the first trip to Mars.
## BONUS — APOLLO COMMAND MODULE PILOT
23. And a bonus, from history. The Apollo command module pilot. In nineteen sixty-nine, while two astronauts walked on the Moon, Michael Collins circled above it, alone.
24. Every time he passed behind the Moon, he lost radio contact with Earth for about forty-seven minutes. A NASA commentator said: not since Adam has any human known such solitude. But Collins wrote that he didn't feel lonely. He felt awareness, confidence, almost joy.
## WHY PEOPLE DO IT
25. So why would anyone choose this? People who do these jobs often talk about the same things. Quiet. Focus. And a view that almost nobody else on Earth gets to see.
26. Space agencies study these crews closely, because the first people going to Mars will face the same thing. And maybe there's a lesson for the rest of us too. Loneliness isn't only about distance. It's about having nobody to talk to.
