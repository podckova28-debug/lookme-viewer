# Why It's So Hard to Be a Delivery Courier — Episode 2 (~6 min, 30 blocks)

Style: Dinzo. White background, flat vector, second person, deadpan. Narrator: Grady (ElevenLabs), tempo x1.2.

## COLD OPEN
1. Nine forty-seven p.m. It's raining sideways. You are on the fourteenth floor of a building whose elevator has been "under maintenance" since spring, holding a bag that is dripping. Inside the bag is soup.
2. You knock. Nothing. You knock again. A voice from behind the door says: "Just leave it." You look at the soup. The soup looks at you. Neither of you is going to talk about the stairs.
## REWIND
3. You've been on the bike for eleven hours. To understand how you ended up here, with soup, go back to this morning, when the app said it was going to be a great day.
## CHAPTER 1 — MORNING
4. Seven thirty. You press "Go online." That's the whole onboarding. No manager, no desk, no colleagues. Just a green button and a bag the size of a small fridge strapped to your back.
5. The first order comes in nine seconds later. Two coffees. Four kilometers. The app shows you the money, and the money is fine. The money is always fine until you divide it by the minutes.
6. You arrive at the café. The coffees are not ready. You stand in the corner by the napkins, the way couriers stand: not in the way, but not quite welcome either. The clock is running. Your pay is not.
7. Eleven minutes later, the coffees are ready. You ride. You deliver. The customer says "thanks" without looking up. The app asks you to rate the experience. You give it five stars. You always give five stars.
8. By ten a.m., you've done six orders and your legs have found their rhythm. This is the good part of the day. Remember it. You'll need it around four.
## CHAPTER 2 — LUNCH RUSH
9. Twelve o'clock. The map turns red. Every restaurant in the city has decided to cook at the same time, and every person in the city has decided they can't walk two hundred meters.
10. The app starts stacking: pick up here, then here, deliver there, then there. Four bags, two hands, one bike. You become a very fast game of Tetris with a heartbeat.
11. Order two is on the fifth floor. "Leave at the door," the note says. You leave it at the door. You're back on the bike when the phone rings. "Where's my order?" At the door. "Which door?" Yours.
12. Order three is a family-size pizza. Pizza is the enemy. Pizza is flat, wide, and takes the full bag. Every bump in the road is a decision about where the cheese ends up.
13. Order four tipped in the app. Order four's tip is listed as "pending." It will stay pending. You've stopped checking. The app knows this about you, and it's fine with it.
14. One thirty. The red fades. You've done fourteen orders in ninety minutes, and the city goes back to pretending it doesn't need you. Until dinner.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE DEAD HOURS
15. Three p.m. is nothing. No orders. You sit on your bike in a parking lot with eleven other people on their bikes in the same parking lot, all looking at the same screen, all waiting.
16. Your phone is at thirty-one percent. Your phone is your job. If it dies, you don't exist. You have a power bank, a second power bank, and a cable held together with tape.
17. Now, the question nobody asks: where do you go to the bathroom? In one survey of New York couriers, most said restaurants simply never let them in. So the answer is: a gas station. Far away.
18. You do the math on a napkin. Hours online: seven and a half. Money earned: not bad. Money per hour of being actually paid: a different number. You fold the napkin and put it away.
19. Four p.m. The sky changes its mind. You feel the first drop on your helmet, and somewhere in the city, ten thousand people decide that tonight, they're not cooking.
## CHAPTER 4 — RAIN
20. Rain means orders. Rain means money. Rain also means wet gloves, fogged glasses, and brakes that work on a slight delay. The app adds a small bonus. The road adds a small risk. Nobody tells you which is bigger.
21. The bag is waterproof, mostly. Your jacket is waterproof, mostly. You are waterproof, not at all. By six, there is a river running down your spine into places a river shouldn't go.
22. Seven p.m. A customer messages: "Can you hurry?" You are on a bike, in the rain, at a red light. You type "On my way." You've typed "On my way" so many times your phone suggests it before you start.
23. Eight p.m. The building with no elevator again. Different customer, same stairs. Fourteen floors. You've started counting them. Not the steps. The floors. The steps would be depressing.
24. Your rating today is four point nine. Yesterday it was five. Somebody gave you one star. You'll never know who, or why, and the app won't tell you. It just remembers.
25. Nine forty-seven. Fourteenth floor. The soup. "Just leave it." You leave it. You take the stairs down, because the stairs are the only thing in this job that's always exactly where it said it would be.
## CHAPTER 5 — THE NUMBERS
26. Here's what the day looks like from above: twelve hours online, roughly forty kilometers of city, around forty orders, and one actual meal, eaten standing next to a dumpster that smelled better than it looked.
27. No sick days. No bad-weather days. If the app decides you're done, you're done. One report found that nearly half of the couriers it deactivated were later found to have done nothing wrong. The app does not apologize. It doesn't have the feature.
## CHAPTER 6 — END
28. Eleven forty p.m. You press "Go offline." The app asks you to rate your day. You give it five stars. You always give five stars. Then you ride home, in the dark, with an empty bag that finally weighs nothing.
29. Tomorrow, you'll press the green button again. Not because the app is kind, but because the city will still want its soup, and you are the only one who knows about the stairs.
30. That was one shift. Next time: a different job, a different door. Subscribe if you want to be there. The soup, probably, will still be waiting.

Stats used (3):
- Bathroom access: "Essential but Unprotected: App-based Food Couriers in NYC" (Los Deliveristas Unidos / Cornell ILR, 2021, n=500): most respondents reported restaurants never let them use bathrooms. https://www.issuelab.org/resources/38934/38934.pdf
- Deactivation: Human Rights Watch, "The Gig Trap" (2025): nearly half of previously deactivated respondents said the company later found no wrongdoing. https://www.hrw.org/report/2025/05/12/the-gig-trap
- Unpaid waiting / idle time: NYC DCWP administrative data (via Chamber of Progress, 2025), on-call waiting hours counted as unpaid time. https://progresschamber.org/research/nyc-delivery-worker-pay-access-tradeoff/
"Forty kilometers / forty orders" is a narrative estimate, not a sourced statistic.
