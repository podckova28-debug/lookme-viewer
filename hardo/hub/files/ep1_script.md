# Why It's So Hard to Be a Nurse — pilot v2 (denser, Dinzo pacing)

1. Three forty in the morning. Room four has stopped breathing, and you're standing in the corridor holding a coffee that isn't even yours. You put it down. Gently. It's the calmest thing you'll do tonight.
2. You've been awake for twenty hours. This is your third night in a row, and your body has stopped asking questions. To understand how you got here, rewind to seven p.m. You were smiling then.
3. Handover. The day nurse hands you five patients, a list longer than your arm, and a printer that chews the medication sheet. Every night. You've named the printer. You don't say the name out loud.
4. On paper, the shift is twelve hours. In practice, there's charting, there's the family who read something online, and there's the patient who needs you at seven oh one. Nobody counts that hour. You do.
5. You're entitled to a thirty-minute break. Seven out of ten nurses regularly skip it. Tonight you're one of them: a granola bar, standing up, in a supply closet, between two boxes of gloves.
6. The bathroom is thirty seconds away. You pass it going to room two. You pass it coming back. You'll pass it fourteen times before you go in. Your bladder has filed a formal complaint.
7. Midnight. Room two rings every ten minutes: water, pillow, the TV remote, water again. Room four never rings. Not once. And somewhere in your tired brain, that's the one that bothers you.
8. Three a.m. is when the human body most wants to sleep. By some cosmic joke, it's also when everyone on the floor needs you at once. Nobody designed this. It just happens. To you. Every night.
9. Three forty. Room four. You put down the coffee and you run, and for the next ninety seconds nothing else in the world exists. That part of the story is next. You already know you'll be there.

Statistics kept: "seven out of ten nurses skip the break" (JONA 2023 / Magnet study), "twelve-hour shift" (standard). Everything else is narrative time, not data.
