# Why It's So Hard to Be a Line Cook — Episode 4 (~6 min, 30 blocks)

Style: Dinzo. White background, flat vector, second person, deadpan. Narrator: Grady (ElevenLabs), tempo x1.2.

## COLD OPEN
1. Nine fourteen p.m. Saturday. The ticket printer has not stopped in forty minutes. There are forty-one tickets on the rail. Table twelve wants the salmon medium-well, and you did not know salmon had a medium-well.
2. Your left forearm has a new burn from six forty. You don't remember getting it. The burn is shaped like the edge of a sauté pan, because it is the edge of a sauté pan.
## REWIND
3. You've been standing since one p.m. To understand how you ended up here, holding a fish and an opinion, rewind to the afternoon, when the kitchen was quiet and you still had a plan.
## CHAPTER 1 — PREP
4. One p.m. The kitchen is cold, the radio is on, and there are forty pounds of onions. You dice all of them. Your eyes stop crying after bag two. That is not a metaphor. Your eyes just gave up.
5. Prep is the part nobody sees. Sauces, stocks, portioned proteins, herbs picked leaf by leaf. Every plate tonight was built now, in silence, by a guy in a backwards cap with a very sharp knife.
6. The walk-in cooler is thirty-eight degrees. It is also the only place in the building where you can stand still for ten seconds. People go in for lemons. People stay for the silence.
7. Three thirty. Family meal: the staff eats together, standing, out of a hotel pan. It is the best meal you'll have today. It is also the only one. You eat it in four minutes, like a raccoon with a schedule.
## CHAPTER 2 — DOORS
8. Five p.m. Doors. The first ticket prints. It makes a small, polite sound. By seven, it will sound like a machine gun that hates you personally.
9. The line is six feet wide and about four hundred degrees. Grill, sauté, fryer, the pass. Four people, no room, one rule: say where you are. "Behind." "Hot." "Corner." You will say "behind" two hundred times tonight. Nobody has ever said it back to you at home.
10. Seven forty. The rush. Tickets come faster than plates leave. You have six pans on four burners, which is a math problem you solve with your hands. Everything is either burning or not ready. There is no third state.
11. A ticket comes back. Table twelve. The salmon was, and this is a quote, "too fishy." You refire it. You smile at the fish. The fish understands.
12. There is an allergy ticket. Severe. Nuts. You change gloves, change the board, change the pan, change the tongs. You do it in ninety seconds, and you do it right, because this is the one ticket tonight that isn't about dinner.
13. "Eighty-six the lamb." Which means the lamb is gone. Eleven tables ordered it anyway. Each server comes back to tell you the news personally. You write it on the board. The board does not help.
14. You do not go to the bathroom. If this sounds familiar, you've met the nurse, the courier and the teacher. Same bladder, hotter room.
## CHAPTER 3 — THE BODY
15. Here is what the hands pay. In full-service restaurants, cuts bad enough to miss work happen about three times as often as across all private industry. Burns, almost seven times as often. That's the Bureau of Labor Statistics, not the chef on TV.
16. You have a burn from tonight, a cut from Tuesday, and a scar from a different restaurant that you point to like a souvenir. Cooks compare them. Nobody wins. Everyone has a story about a fryer.
17. Your feet have been on a rubber mat for eight hours. Your knees have an opinion. Your back has a petition. You are thirty-one years old and you walk like a chair.
## CHAPTER 4 — THE MONEY
18. The median cook in America makes about seventeen dollars and nineteen cents an hour. That's thirty-five thousand seven hundred sixty a year, before the burns. The chef on TV makes that in one episode, and he doesn't do dishes.
19. Tips go to the front of the house. You are the back of the house. The wall between you is three feet thick and made of a heat lamp. Every night, somebody out there thanks the server for the sauce you made at two p.m.
20. People leave this industry fast. In food service, about one in eighteen workers leaves their job every month. Across the whole economy, it's about one in thirty-three. Over a year, that is most of the kitchen, gone and replaced.
## CHAPTER 5 — THE LAST TICKET
21. Ten thirty. Last ticket. Table twelve, again, because of course. Dessert. You plate it like it's the first one tonight, because the person eating it doesn't know it's the last.
22. Then the clean-down. You scrape the flattop, pull the fryer oil, bleach the boards, wrap forty containers, and label every one with tape and a marker in handwriting only you can read.
23. Eleven forty. The floor gets mopped. The mop gets mopped. The music is yours now, and it's loud, and for eleven minutes the kitchen is the best place on earth.
24. The chef says "good push." Two words. You'll run them back in your head on the bus. You will not tell anyone that you did that.
25. Twelve twenty a.m. The bus. You smell like fryer oil and lemons. The person next to you moves one seat over. You understand. You would also move.
26. One ten a.m. Home. You eat cereal standing at the counter, because sitting feels like a decision. You watch a cooking show. You yell at it. You go to bed at two.
## CHAPTER 6 — WHY
27. So why do it. Not the money. Not the hours. Not the burns. It's the eleven minutes with the mop and the music. It's "good push." It's a plate leaving the pass exactly the way you saw it in your head.
28. And it's the people. The four of you, six feet of steel, four hundred degrees, two hundred "behinds," and not one dropped plate. Nobody films that part either. It would not go viral.
29. Tomorrow starts at one p.m. There will be onions. There will be a table twelve. There is always a table twelve. You already know what you'll say to the fish.
30. That was one day. Next time: a different job, a different door. Subscribe if you want to be there. And please, for everyone's sake, don't order the salmon medium-well.
